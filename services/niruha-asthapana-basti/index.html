Niruha / Asthapana Basti in Patna

Niruha, also called asthapana basti, is a cleansing enema made from a carefully emulsified mixture of honey, rock salt, medicated oil, herbal paste and decoction, given on an empty stomach and expelled within a short time. It is the cleansing partner of the oil enema in a classical basti course, used mainly for vata disorders, and is planned and supervised by a doctor.

The preparation of a classical niruha basti is one of the small rituals of a Panchakarma kitchen. Honey goes into the mortar first, then rock salt, then warm oil, then the herbal paste, and finally the warm decoction, each added slowly and churned until the mixture becomes a uniform, frothy emulsion. Get the order wrong and it separates. The texts specify this sequence because the formulation has to behave properly inside the body.

What the names mean

Niruha (निरूह) comes from nir-uh, to remove or eliminate: it removes doshas and waste. Asthapana (आस्थापन) means that which establishes or stabilises: it is said to stabilise age and strength. Both names describe the same procedure, a decoction-based enema, and it is also called kashaya basti after its main ingredient, the decoction.

The classical record

The Siddhi Sthana of the Charaka Samhita devotes several chapters to basti, including the preparation of niruha, the proportions of ingredients, the qualities of the nozzle and bag, positions, timing and complications. The famous statement that basti is half of all therapy appears in the Charaka tradition, and Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya repeats it. The Sushruta Samhita lists many basti formulations. Named preparations such as erandamuladi niruha (based on castor root), dashamula niruha and madhutailika basti (a milder honey-oil form) come from these texts.

Course structures are also classical: karma basti of 30 enemas, kala basti of 16 and yoga basti of 8, alternating oil and decoction enemas in set patterns. Modern Panchakarma departments have standardised preparation and monitoring, while keeping the classical ratios.

How it is understood to work

Ayurvedic reasoning

Vata is described as the driver of all movement in the body, including the movement of the other two doshas. The colon is its main seat. A decoction enema, according to the texts, draws out doshas and accumulated waste from the whole body through the colon, a little like roots of a tree drawing nourishment upward but in reverse. The ingredients each have a role: honey to penetrate and carry, rock salt to liquefy and draw, oil to protect the bowel lining and settle vata, paste to add specific herbal action, and the decoction as the main medicine.

A properly given niruha is expected to come out within a short time, classically within about 48 minutes (one muhurta), along with stool and flatus, leaving the person feeling light, with good appetite and clear senses.

What research shows

Formal research is limited and mostly small. A study in the Indian Journal of Medical Research (2015) examined a structured basti course in obese volunteers and reported changes in immune and metabolic markers. A 2020 pilot study in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine compared a lekhana (scraping) basti with nasal therapy in obesity. Single-arm studies in low back pain and other vata conditions report improvement, but without control groups it is impossible to separate the effect of basti from time, rest and other treatments. We describe niruha as a classical procedure with promising early research, not as a proven cure.

Adapting niruha to the person

Although basti is the principal therapy for vata, the formulation can be shifted toward other doshas. For pitta involvement, cooling decoctions and milk-based preparations may be used. For kapha and metabolic concerns, scraping (lekhana) formulations with drier, more penetrating herbs are chosen. For depleted, weak patients, nourishing (brimhana) bastis with milk and nourishing herbs (the texts also describe meat broths) may be preferred. Some conditions call for ksheera basti, where the decoction is prepared in milk. This flexibility is part of what makes basti central to classical practice, and also why it must be prescribed rather than chosen from a menu.

Volume is individual too. The classical texts give different quantities by age and strength, and a doctor will usually begin conservatively, watching how the first enema is retained and expelled before settling on the course dose.

Living alongside a basti course

Many patients in Patna attend a basti course while staying at home. That works well if daily routine supports it: a light breakfast on anuvasana days and an empty stomach on niruha days, no long two-wheeler rides or travel on treatment days, an early dinner, and no heavy exercise. Students and working people often schedule courses around holidays so that these rules are easier to follow.

Who may be considered

  • Vata-dominant chronic conditions, as judged by the doctor, often involving the back, joints, nerves and bowel, alongside conventional care where needed.
  • Some metabolic presentations, using scraping formulations, after assessment.
  • Patients undergoing a classical alternating basti course.

Who should not receive niruha basti

  • Immediately after a meal; niruha is given on an empty stomach.
  • Acute diarrhoea, dysentery, rectal bleeding, active piles, fissures or fistula, rectal prolapse, active inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Recent abdominal or anorectal surgery, intestinal obstruction, abdominal masses.
  • Severe weakness, emaciation, dehydration, acute fever.
  • Pregnancy.
  • Severe heart disease, uncontrolled diabetes or kidney disease, unless specifically planned with full assessment.
  • Persistent cough, breathlessness, hiccups or vomiting at the time.

Never stop prescribed medicines for a basti course. Medicines for diabetes and blood pressure need particular attention on niruha days because of the empty stomach and fluid shifts, so tell the doctor everything you take.

What happens at Sabka Ved Wellness

Planning the course

After examination, Dr. Aditya Kunal decides the formulation, the volume, the number of bastis and the alternating pattern with anuvasana. A course is reviewed every day: appetite, stool, energy, sleep and any discomfort guide the next step.

Preparation of the enema

The decoction is prepared fresh from crude herbs. The ingredients are combined in the classical order and churned to a uniform warm emulsion just before use. This takes time and skill, and it is one of the reasons niruha cannot be prepared hours in advance or reused.

The procedure, described for understanding

  1. You come in on an empty stomach, having passed stool and urine if possible.
  2. Abhyanga of the lower back, abdomen and thighs and gentle swedana are given.
  3. You lie on your left side with the right knee drawn up.
  4. The trained staff member slowly introduces the warm emulsion through a lubricated soft catheter, without letting air enter.
  5. You turn onto your back and rest, while staff note the time.
  6. When the urge comes, you pass the enema in a private attached toilet. The time of expulsion and its character are recorded and reported to the doctor.
  7. After expulsion, you rest and later take a warm bath and a light meal such as rice with thin soup or milk, as advised.

When things do not go as expected

If the enema is not expelled within the expected time, the doctor may use warmth, gentle measures or a further mild basti. Cramping, discomfort or weakness are reported straight away. These situations are part of why basti is a supervised procedure.

Dignity and hygiene

All basti procedures are done privately by trained staff of the same gender, with single-use catheters and fresh linen, and a clean attached toilet. You may ask questions or pause at any time.

Why the cost reflects real work

Each niruha requires fresh decoction from crude herbs, honey, medicated oil and paste prepared on the day, skilled emulsification, preparatory abhyanga and swedana, sterile disposables, private rooms with toilets, and daily doctor review across a course that may run for a week, two weeks or a month. In summary

Niruha basti is not a cure for any disease. It is one of the central procedures of classical Panchakarma for vata conditions, and it needs careful selection and supervision. FAQ

Questions about Niruha / Asthapana Basti

What is the difference between niruha and anuvasana basti?

Niruha is a larger decoction-based cleansing enema given on an empty stomach and expelled within a short time. Anuvasana is a smaller oil enema given after food and retained for hours. They are alternated in classical courses.

How long does niruha take to come out?

Classically, a properly given niruha is expelled within about 48 minutes. The doctor is informed if it takes much longer or comes out too quickly.

What is yoga basti, kala basti and karma basti?

They are classical course lengths of 8, 16 and 30 enemas, alternating oil and decoction bastis in set patterns. The doctor chooses based on your condition.

Can I eat before a niruha basti?

No. Niruha is given on an empty stomach. Light food is taken after the enema has been expelled.

Is basti safe for people with piles?

Active, bleeding or painful piles, fissures and fistula are reasons to avoid it. The doctor examines and decides.
