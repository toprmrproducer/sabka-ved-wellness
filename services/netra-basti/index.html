Netra Basti / Akshi Tarpana pooling in Patna

Netra basti, the pooling form of akshi tarpana, is a classical eye therapy in which a ring of dough is built around each eye and filled with lukewarm medicated ghee, in which the patient gently opens and closes the eyes for a set time. It is used for eye strain, dryness and fatigue as part of a plan, after a doctor confirms there is no infection or condition that needs an eye specialist first.

Ask an office worker, a student preparing for UPSC or BPSC, or a gamer what their eyes feel like at the end of the day, and you will hear the same words: dry, gritty, heavy, burning, tired. Screens have made eye strain one of the most common complaints we hear. Netra basti is one of the classical therapies people ask about for it, and it is one of the more unusual experiences in Ayurveda: lying back with your eyes open in a small pool of warm ghee.

What it is called and why

Netra (नेत्र) and akshi (अक्षि) both mean eye. Tarpana (तर्पण) means nourishing or satisfying. Basti here means a reservoir. Akshi tarpana is the classical name for nourishing the eyes by holding medicated ghee over them. In many clinics, the pooling technique is also called netra basti, because a small reservoir is built around the eye. The same therapy is described on our netra tarpana page from the perspective of eye-specific (Shalakya) treatment.

History in the texts

Unlike the knee and back bastis, this therapy is clearly described in the classical literature. The Sushruta Samhita, whose Uttara Tantra contains an extensive section on eye diseases, describes tarpana in detail, as does Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya in its section on eye treatments (kriyakalpa). The texts specify building a wall of black gram dough around the eye, filling it with lukewarm, liquefied ghee up to the level of the eyelashes, the duration in terms of counted syllables (matra) depending on the condition, the opening and closing of the eyes, and the signs of proper, insufficient and excessive tarpana. Kriyakalpa, the group of local eye procedures that includes tarpana, putapaka, seka, aschyotana and anjana, remains a core part of Ayurvedic ophthalmology teaching today.

How it is understood to work

Classical reasoning

The eyes are described as the seat of alochaka pitta, the aspect of pitta responsible for vision, and as particularly vulnerable to vata's dryness and pitta's heat. Overuse, strain, smoke, dust, heat and late nights are said to dry and inflame the eyes. Medicated ghee is chosen because ghee is considered the best substance to nourish and cool while carrying herbal properties. Triphala ghrita, jeevantyadi ghrita, mahatriphala ghrita and plain go-ghrita are commonly used, with the choice depending on the condition.

What research shows

Studies are small. A 2012 pilot study published in Ayu tested an Ayurvedic approach to computer vision syndrome in thirty patients, with groups receiving oral herbal medicine plus tarpana with cow's ghee, tarpana alone, or counselling about working conditions, and reported symptomatic improvements; the authors called it a pilot study. Other small comparative studies with medicated ghees have reported relief of eye strain symptoms. These are preliminary findings. There is no evidence that tarpana corrects refractive error, reverses cataract or glaucoma, or treats retinal disease, and we make no such claims.

Duration and signs the texts describe

The classical texts measure tarpana time in matra, a unit based on counting syllables, and give different durations for different conditions: shorter for healthy eyes and mild complaints, longer for conditions involving deeper structures. Modern practice translates this into a few minutes per eye, adjusted by the doctor. The signs of proper tarpana listed in the texts include comfortable sleep, easy waking, clear vision, the ability to tolerate light, a feeling of lightness and freshness in the eyes. Excess tarpana is said to cause heaviness, stickiness, watering and itching; insufficient tarpana leaves dryness, roughness and difficulty with light. Staff watch for these signs and the doctor adjusts the remaining sessions.

Eyes in the Patna environment

Winter smog, roadside dust, smoke from cooking fires and crop burning, and the glare of summer all trouble the eyes in the Gangetic plain. Ayurveda would describe these as aggravating pitta and vata in the eyes. Netra basti is often scheduled in the cooler months, and patients are advised to protect their eyes with sunglasses and to avoid rubbing them.

Who may be considered

  • Eye strain and fatigue from prolonged screen use or reading.
  • Mild dryness and grittiness, after an eye check excludes conditions needing other treatment.
  • Burning and heaviness of the eyes linked to heat, dust and smoke exposure.
  • Selected patients as part of a wider plan for head and eye complaints.

Who should not have netra basti

  • Any eye infection, conjunctivitis, styes, discharge or redness of uncertain cause.
  • Recent eye surgery, including cataract or LASIK, unless the eye surgeon permits.
  • Glaucoma, uveitis, retinal detachment or any condition under active ophthalmic treatment, unless coordinated with the eye specialist.
  • Sudden loss or blurring of vision, eye pain, flashes of light or new floaters: these need urgent eye examination.
  • Contact lens wear on the day (lenses must be removed and not reinserted until advised).
  • Acute cold, fever, sinus infection or headache.
  • Extreme fatigue, very hot or very cold weather, or immediately after a heavy meal, as classically advised.

Continue eye drops and medicines prescribed by your ophthalmologist. Do not stop glaucoma drops under any circumstances without your eye doctor's advice.

What happens at Sabka Ved Wellness

Assessment

Dr. Aditya Kunal asks about your symptoms, screen time, sleep, spectacles and any eye conditions or surgery, and examines the eyes externally. If anything suggests infection, raised eye pressure or retinal disease, he will refer you to an ophthalmologist first. He selects the ghee and the number of sessions.

The session, described for understanding

  1. A gentle face massage and warm towel or mild steam may be given first, as some protocols advise, to prepare the area.
  2. You lie on your back. A ring of black gram dough is built around each eye, sealed to the skin so it will not leak.
  3. Medicated ghee is melted and cooled to lukewarm; its temperature is checked carefully, because the eye is sensitive.
  4. With the eyes closed, the ghee is poured slowly into each ring up to the level of the lashes.
  5. You are then asked to open and close the eyes slowly and gently, rolling the eyeballs, so the ghee bathes the surface. Mild blurring and a slight sting at first are normal.
  6. After the set time, the ghee is drained from a small opening made at the outer corner, the dough removed, and the eyes gently cleaned.
  7. Warm water fomentation and, in some protocols, a brief herbal smoke inhalation follow to clear residual heaviness.

After the session

Vision may remain blurred for a while because of the ghee film, so do not drive or ride a two-wheeler immediately afterwards; arrange to be accompanied. Avoid bright sunlight, screens, wind, dust and smoke for the rest of the day, and sleep early. Wear sunglasses outdoors. Report pain, increasing redness or reduced vision immediately.

Habits that protect your eyes

For screen-related eye strain, simple habits matter as much as any therapy: the 20-20-20 rule (every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds), blinking consciously, keeping the screen slightly below eye level, adequate room lighting, and regular eye checks for spectacles. We teach these alongside any course of netra basti.

What the cost covers

Classical medicated ghees for the eyes are prepared through lengthy processes and must be of high quality and clean. Fresh dough and fresh ghee are used for each patient, never reused. A trained therapist stays throughout to manage temperature and comfort, and the doctor's screening ensures it is safe for you. We do not publish prices online; the plan and costs are explained at consultation.

A careful conclusion

Netra basti is not a cure for any eye disease and it does not replace your ophthalmologist. It is a classical nourishing therapy that may relieve strain and dryness for suitable patients. FAQ

Questions about Netra Basti / Akshi Tarpana pooling

Do I have to keep my eyes open in the ghee?

You are asked to open and close your eyes slowly and gently so the ghee bathes the eye surface. A slight sting and blurring at first are normal and usually settle quickly.

Can netra basti remove my spectacles?

No. There is no evidence that tarpana corrects refractive error. It is used for strain, dryness and fatigue.

Can I drive home after the session?

It is better not to. Vision can stay blurred for a while because of the ghee film. Arrange for someone to accompany you.

Is it safe after cataract surgery?

Only when your eye surgeon permits. Recent eye surgery is a reason to avoid it until healing is complete.

How many sessions are usually advised?

Commonly 3, 5 or 7 consecutive days, depending on the condition, as decided after assessment.
