पुटपाक eye therapy with herbal juices

Putapaka in Patna

Putapaka is a classical Ayurvedic eye procedure, one of the kriyakalpa, in which a juice extracted from herbs that have been wrapped in leaves, sealed in clay and heated (the puta method) is held over the open eye inside a dough ring, much like tarpana. Classically it follows a course of netra tarpana. It is a specialised procedure used selectively, after an eye examination.

If Netra tarpana is the better known of the classical eye baths, Putapaka is its lesser-known partner. The two are described side by side in the texts, and the classical sequence is tarpana first, putapaka after. Where tarpana bathes the eye in medicated ghee, putapaka uses a herbal juice prepared by an old and labour-intensive method that gives the therapy its name. Few clinics offer it today, partly because of that preparation and partly because it needs careful judgement. This page explains what it is so that you can understand it if it is ever suggested.

What Putapaka is

Puta refers to a sealed covering or enclosure, and paka means cooking. In the putapaka method of preparing medicine, fresh herbs, and in some classical formulas other substances, are pounded, wrapped in layers of leaves such as lotus, castor or banana, coated with clay and heated in a fire until the clay turns reddish. The bundle is then opened, and the juice is squeezed out and strained. This juice, sometimes mixed with ghee or other liquids, is used for the eye procedure.

The procedure itself resembles tarpana: a ring of black gram dough is built around the eye, the lukewarm juice is poured in, and the patient opens and closes the eyes gently for a set time before it is drained.

The texts describe three types:

  • Snehana: unctuous, for vata and dryness
  • Lekhana: scraping or cleansing, for kapha and heaviness
  • Prasadana or ropana: soothing and healing, for pitta, blood and inflammation-related complaints

Classical history

Putapaka is described in Sushruta Samhita's Uttaratantra and in Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya (around 7th century CE), within the kriyakalpa, the local eye therapies. These texts give the method of preparation, the three types, the duration for each, the order in which tarpana and putapaka should follow each other, the aftercare, such as avoiding bright light and wind, and the signs of correct and excessive treatment. The rationale given is that putapaka restores strength to the eye after tarpana, which may leave the eye feeling heavy.

The same word, puta, is also used in Rasa Shastra, Ayurvedic pharmaceutics, for the heating process used to make mineral preparations such as bhasma. That is a different use of the same technique and should not be confused with the eye therapy.

How it is understood to work, and what evidence exists

Classical reasoning

The sealed cooking is believed to concentrate the juice and soften its sharpness, making it suitable for contact with the eye. Each type of putapaka uses herbs with qualities that counter the dosha involved. After tarpana nourishes the eye with ghee, putapaka is thought to balance any heaviness and support clear vision.

What research shows

We found very little modern research on putapaka as a distinct eye procedure. It appears in some Ayurvedic case reports of eye disease alongside tarpana and other treatments, such as a 2019 case report on ulcerative keratitis in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine, which described a combination of Ayurvedic local and internal measures. Trials on tarpana in dry eye, such as a 2020 randomised comparative trial in Ayu, give context for the family of procedures but are not evidence for putapaka itself. Honestly put: putapaka rests largely on classical description and specialist clinical experience, and needs proper study.

Who may be considered

Only after an eye examination, and usually as part of a course that begins with tarpana:

  • Some patients with chronic dry, strained eyes who have had netra tarpana
  • Selected chronic eye complaints, as supportive care alongside an ophthalmologist

Who must not have it

  • Any acute eye infection, conjunctivitis, red painful eye or discharge
  • Eye injury or chemical exposure, which needs emergency treatment
  • Recent eye surgery, unless your eye surgeon specifically agrees
  • Sudden change in vision, eye pain, haloes or flashes of light
  • Acute cold, fever or indigestion, as the texts advise
  • Known allergy to any ingredient

Putapaka does not treat glaucoma, cataract, retinal disease or refractive errors. Never stop eye drops your ophthalmologist has prescribed, and never put herbal juices into your eyes at home. Home-made juices are not sterile and can cause serious infection.

Preparation

The herbs are prepared by the puta method on the day of use, with strict attention to cleanliness, then strained carefully and warmed to a lukewarm temperature.

The procedure, for understanding

  1. You lie on your back. The face may be gently massaged and warmed.
  2. A dough ring is sealed around the eye.
  3. The lukewarm putapaka liquid is poured into the ring until the lashes are covered.
  4. You open and close the eyes slowly for the time set by the doctor.
  5. The liquid is drained, the ring removed and the eyes cleaned.
  6. You rest with eyes closed in a dim room for a while before leaving.

Aftercare

The texts are firm about aftercare: avoid bright light, wind, dust, smoke, screens and late nights for the rest of the day, and do not look at the sun or fire. Wear dark glasses outdoors. Do not drive immediately. Eat light, warm food.

Why a rare therapy still deserves an honest page

People increasingly search for unusual Ayurvedic eye therapies after seeing them online, sometimes in videos that show herbal juices or ghee being poured into eyes at home or at wellness camps. Some of these are harmless; others are risky. The eye has very little tolerance for contamination, and an infection of the cornea can threaten sight within days. That is why we explain putapaka in detail but use it sparingly.

When we do consider it, three things are non-negotiable. First, an eye examination, so that conditions like glaucoma, cataract, retinal disease or infection are not missed. Second, fresh, clean preparation on the day, with careful straining. Third, a doctor present throughout, ready to stop if there is any discomfort. If those conditions cannot be met, the safer choice is not to do it.

For most people with tired or dry eyes, the useful steps are much simpler: screen breaks, enough sleep, correct glasses, sunglasses in bright light, treating acidity and constipation, and, where suitable, netra tarpana. Putapaka is a specialist's tool, not a routine treatment.

Why it costs what it does

Putapaka is labour-intensive. The herbs must be prepared fresh by sealed cooking, which takes time, skill and fuel, and the procedure needs close one-to-one supervision because it involves the eye. It is usually part of a course with tarpana. Fixed prices are not published, but you will know what your plan includes before you begin.

FAQ

Questions about Putapaka

What is Putapaka?

Putapaka is a classical Ayurvedic eye procedure in which a herbal juice, prepared by cooking herbs sealed in leaves and clay, is held over the open eye inside a dough ring.

How is Putapaka different from Netra Tarpana?

Tarpana uses medicated ghee and comes first. Putapaka uses a specially prepared herbal juice and classically follows tarpana.

Is there research on Putapaka?

Very little. It rests mainly on classical texts and specialist experience, and needs proper clinical study.

Can I make putapaka juice at home?

No. Home-made juices are not sterile and can seriously harm the eye. Nothing should be put in the eyes without a prescription.

What should I avoid after Putapaka?

Bright light, wind, dust, smoke, screens and late nights for the rest of the day, as the classical texts advise.
