Kavala in Patna

Kavala, or kavala graha, is a classical Ayurvedic mouth therapy in which a moderate amount of medicated liquid, such as oil, decoction or herbal water, is swished actively around the mouth and then spat out. It is used for oral hygiene, gum care, dryness of the mouth and some throat complaints, as an addition to, not a replacement for, dental care.

Oil pulling became a worldwide wellness trend a few years ago, with celebrities swishing coconut oil on social media. Most of them did not know they were practising, loosely, one of Ayurveda's two classical mouth therapies. The texts distinguish carefully between them. In gandusha, the mouth is filled so fully that the liquid cannot move, and it is simply held. In kavala, a smaller amount is taken so that it can be moved freely, swished around the teeth and gums and gargled before being spat out. Modern oil pulling is much closer to kavala.

What Kavala is

Kavala means a mouthful, and kavala graha means holding and moving a mouthful. The liquid may be:

  • A medicated oil or plain sesame oil, for dryness and to strengthen the gums and teeth
  • A warm herbal decoction, such as triphala, for cleansing and for gums that bleed easily
  • Honey water, milk or ghee-based preparations for specific conditions
  • A paste dissolved in liquid, called kalka kavala, for particular complaints

The amount is chosen so that it can move comfortably in the mouth. It is swished between the teeth, around the gums and cheeks, and sometimes gargled at the back of the throat, before being spat out.

Classical history

Ashtanga Hridaya (around 7th century CE), in the Sutrasthana, gives a dedicated chapter to gandusha and kavala. It describes four types by purpose: snigdha or snaihika (unctuous, for vata), shamana (palliative, for pitta), shodhana (cleansing, for kapha) and ropana (healing, for ulcers and wounds). It explains the difference in quantity between the two methods, the signs that the procedure has been done long enough, and the conditions each suits. Charaka Samhita, in its chapter on daily regimen, praises holding oil in the mouth for strengthening the jaw, voice and teeth and preventing dryness of the throat and cracked lips. Sushruta Samhita also discusses these procedures within the treatment of mouth diseases.

Kavala is therefore a classical practice with a clear textual base, and it has remained part of Indian household habits in simpler forms, such as gargling with warm salt water or turmeric water for a sore throat.

How it is understood to work, and what research shows

The classical view

The mouth is described as a seat of kapha and of bodhaka kapha in particular, which relates to taste. Moving a medicated liquid actively around the mouth is believed to cleanse accumulated kapha, strengthen the gums and teeth, refresh the mouth, improve taste and support the voice. The choice of liquid directs the effect: oils for dryness and vata, cooling liquids for heat and ulcers, astringent decoctions for bleeding or swollen gums.

What modern research shows

Oil pulling, which resembles kavala, has been studied more than most Ayurvedic procedures. A 2009 randomised, triple-blind study in the Indian Journal of Dental Research compared sesame oil pulling with chlorhexidine mouthwash in 20 adolescent boys with plaque-induced gingivitis and found that both groups showed significant reductions in plaque and gingival index scores and in bacterial counts. A 2016 systematic review from Oxford's Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine, covering five small randomised trials, concluded that the limited evidence suggests oil pulling may have beneficial effects on oral hygiene over the short periods studied, but called for more rigorous trials. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis compared oil pulling with chlorhexidine and other mouthwashes. Overall, the evidence supports oil pulling as a possible adjunct for plaque and gum health, not as a replacement for brushing, flossing and dental treatment.

Who may benefit

  • People wanting to support daily oral hygiene alongside brushing
  • Mild gum inflammation and bleeding gums, after a dental check-up
  • Dry mouth and cracked lips, with oil-based kavala
  • Bad breath linked with oral hygiene, once dental and other causes are checked
  • Some throat irritation, using warm decoctions as gargles
  • Mouth ulcers, using healing or cooling preparations prescribed for them

Who should avoid it or take care

  • Young children, who may swallow or inhale the liquid
  • People with swallowing difficulty or a tendency to choke, such as after a stroke
  • Anyone with tooth pain, swelling of the face or gums, loose teeth or pus, who needs a dentist first
  • Persistent mouth ulcers (lasting more than two weeks), white or red patches, or a lump in the mouth, especially in tobacco users, which need urgent evaluation to rule out oral cancer
  • Allergy to any ingredient

Kavala does not treat tooth decay, gum disease, infections or oral cancer. Keep your dental appointments and continue prescribed treatment.

How it is done, for understanding

  1. You sit upright, preferably on an empty stomach in the morning after brushing, or as advised.
  2. A moderate amount of the prescribed liquid, lukewarm if oil or decoction, is taken into the mouth.
  3. It is swished actively around the teeth, gums and cheeks, and gargled at the back of the throat if advised, without swallowing.
  4. After the time advised, it is spat out, not into a sink that clogs but into a bin or tissue if it is oil.
  5. The mouth is rinsed with warm water.

The texts describe signs that the procedure is complete, such as a feeling of lightness in the mouth and clearing of the senses. In practice the time is set by the doctor.

Aftercare

Do not eat for a short while afterwards. Keep up brushing twice a day and cleaning between the teeth. Reduce sugary drinks, frequent tea with sugar, and especially tobacco, gutkha and paan masala, which are among the most damaging habits for the mouth.

Gutkha, paan and the mouths we see in Bihar

It would be dishonest to write about mouth care in Bihar without mentioning tobacco. Khaini, gutkha, paan with tobacco and cigarettes are common in our region, and they are the leading cause of oral cancer in India. They also stain teeth, damage gums, cause burning and stiffness of the mouth lining (oral submucous fibrosis) and reduce how far the mouth can open. No gargle undoes that damage. If you use tobacco in any form, the most powerful thing you can do for your mouth is to stop, and we will support you in that.

Anyone who uses tobacco and notices a white or red patch, a non-healing ulcer, a lump, difficulty opening the mouth or a burning sensation with spicy food should see a doctor or dentist promptly. Early detection makes an enormous difference. Kavala can be part of a healthier routine once those checks are done, but it is never a reason to delay them.

FAQ

Questions about Kavala

What is Kavala in Ayurveda?

Kavala is a classical mouth therapy in which a moderate amount of medicated liquid is swished and gargled in the mouth and then spat out.

Is Kavala the same as oil pulling?

Modern oil pulling is very close to kavala, since the oil is moved around the mouth. Gandusha is different: the mouth is filled completely and the liquid held still.

Does oil pulling really work?

Small trials suggest it may reduce plaque and gum inflammation over short periods, and a 2016 systematic review called the evidence limited but encouraging. It should be used alongside brushing, not instead of it.

Can children do Kavala?

Young children should not, because they may swallow or inhale the liquid.

Can Kavala cure mouth ulcers?

Some preparations may soothe ulcers, but ulcers lasting more than two weeks, or any white or red patch or lump, need urgent medical evaluation.
