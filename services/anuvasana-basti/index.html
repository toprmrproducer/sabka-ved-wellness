Anuvasana Basti in Patna

Anuvasana basti is a small-volume enema of warm medicated oil or ghee, given after a meal and intended to be retained for some hours. It is the oil half of the classical basti course and is used mainly for vata conditions, alternating with decoction enemas, after a doctor's assessment and under supervision.

Classical Ayurveda gives basti a rank above every other procedure. The Charaka Samhita calls it half of all treatment, and some physicians say the whole of it, because vata, the dosha of movement, is involved in most disease, and basti is its principal remedy. Within basti there are two partners. One cleanses with decoction. The other, anuvasana, nourishes and settles with oil.

What anuvasana means

Anuvasana (अनुवासन) is usually explained as 'that which stays' or 'that which can be given daily without harm'. The root vas means to stay or dwell. It is an enema of medicated oil or ghee that is meant to remain in the colon for a period, rather than being expelled at once. Basti (बस्ति) literally means bladder: classical enemas were given using an animal bladder as the reservoir attached to a nozzle.

A gentler, smaller form, given in a fixed low dose and safe for wider daily use, is called matra basti. The larger classical oil enema is sometimes called sneha basti.

History and documentation

The Charaka Samhita covers basti in detail in the Siddhi Sthana, including the equipment, the qualities of a good nozzle, the dose of oil, timing after meals, retention time, signs of proper and improper administration, and the complications of mistakes, with their management. The Sushruta Samhita and Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya describe the same procedures. The classical courses, such as the karma basti (30 enemas), kala basti (16) and yoga basti (8), alternate anuvasana with niruha in set patterns.

In modern practice, the animal bladder has been replaced by sterile disposable enema sets or a syringe with a soft rubber catheter. The principles described in the texts remain the basis of how basti is taught in BAMS and MD Panchakarma courses.

How it is meant to work

The dosha view

Ayurveda places the main seat of vata in the colon (pakvashaya). Dryness, roughness and lightness aggravate vata; oil counters all three. An oil enema is thought to lubricate and nourish the colon directly, settle vata at its root and, through the colon's connections, influence the whole body. It also prevents the dryness and weakness that a cleansing decoction enema alone might cause, which is why anuvasana is always alternated with niruha in classical courses.

The texts state that a properly given anuvasana is ideally retained for a period of hours (classically described in terms of three yamas, about nine hours) and then passes out with flatus and stool without discomfort.

What studies show

Research on basti is growing but still limited. A study published in the Indian Journal of Medical Research in 2015 gave obese volunteers a course of basti that began with a sesame oil anuvasana, and measured immune and metabolic markers before and after, reporting changes in several inflammatory markers; it was a small proof-of-concept study. A 2014 pilot study in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine explored whether anuvasana basti in escalating doses could substitute for oral ghee before vamana and virechana, for patients who cannot tolerate drinking ghee. These are interesting signals from small studies, not proof of efficacy for any disease.

The texts warn that repeated decoction enemas alone can dry and weaken the body, while repeated oil enemas alone can dull digestion and increase kapha. Alternating them is the classical answer. A typical pattern begins and ends with anuvasana, with niruha days placed between, but the exact sequence is designed for each patient.

Season and daily routine

Basti is classically associated with the rainy season, when vata is said to be aggravated. In Patna's monsoon months, digestion often weakens as well, so the doctor pays close attention to appetite before each oil enema and may pause the course on days of indigestion.

Who may be considered

  • Vata-dominant conditions, as judged by the doctor, including some chronic constipation, dryness, joint and back complaints and neuromuscular concerns, alongside other care.
  • Patients receiving a classical basti course, alternating with niruha.
  • Matra basti, in low doses, for selected patients needing gentle long-term vata care.
  • Some patients who cannot tolerate oral snehapana, as a substitute, if the doctor judges it appropriate.

Who should not receive anuvasana basti

  • People with active indigestion, ama (coated tongue, heaviness, poor appetite) or acute fever.
  • Diarrhoea, active inflammatory bowel disease, rectal bleeding, bleeding piles, anal fissure or fistula in active stage, rectal prolapse.
  • Recent rectal or abdominal surgery, intestinal obstruction.
  • Severe anaemia, kapha-dominant obesity with heaviness, uncontrolled diabetes, unless specifically planned.
  • Pregnancy, except specific late-pregnancy protocols decided only by a qualified physician.
  • Those who have not eaten, since classical anuvasana is given after a light meal.

Tell the doctor about all medicines, especially blood thinners. Never stop a prescribed medicine for a basti course.

What happens at Sabka Ved Wellness

Assessment and course design

Dr. Aditya Kunal decides whether basti suits you, which oil or ghee (for example, sahacharadi taila, dhanwantaram taila, ksheerabala taila or a medicated ghee), what dose, and which course pattern. Some patients receive only a short matra basti course; others receive a structured alternating course.

The procedure, described for understanding

  1. You have a light, warm meal at the advised time. The enema is usually given after the meal, not on an empty stomach.
  2. Abhyanga of the lower back, abdomen and legs, followed by gentle swedana, is given first.
  3. You lie on your left side with the left leg straight and the right knee bent toward the chest.
  4. The staff member gently inserts a lubricated soft catheter into the rectum and slowly introduces the measured, body-warm oil.
  5. The catheter is removed. You lie on your back for some time, and the staff may gently pat the soles and buttocks and raise the legs, which the texts describe to help retention.
  6. You rest, and avoid straining to pass the oil.

After the enema

The oil usually passes later with flatus and stool. If it is not passed after an extended time and you feel discomfort, tell the staff; the doctor decides what to do. Eat light, warm food, avoid cold drinks, travel, long sitting, daytime sleep and exertion on basti days.

Privacy and dignity

Basti is an intimate procedure. It is done in a private room by a trained staff member of the same gender, with the body covered except where necessary, and you can ask questions or pause at any point. Single-use catheters and fresh linen are used for every patient.

Why a basti course costs what it does

Each anuvasana uses a measured volume of classical medicated oil or ghee, given body-warm. A course may involve many sessions, each with abhyanga and swedana beforehand. Disposable sterile equipment, private rooms with attached toilets, trained staff, and the doctor's planning and monitoring are all part of it. An honest summary

Anuvasana basti is not a cure for any disease. It is one of the most important classical therapies for vata and is often given alongside niruha basti in a structured course. To find out whether it suits you, request an appointment or book a consultation with Dr. Aditya Kunal online.

FAQ

Questions about Anuvasana Basti

Is anuvasana basti painful?

It should not be painful. You may feel fullness or a mild urge, and the soft catheter can feel unfamiliar. Tell the staff if you feel discomfort.

How long should the oil be retained?

The texts describe retention for several hours. In practice, the doctor considers it satisfactory if the oil passes later without discomfort. Very quick expulsion or prolonged retention with discomfort is reported to the doctor.

Why is it given after food?

Classical rules say oil enemas are given after a light meal, so that the oil does not move too high or cause discomfort on an empty stomach.

What is matra basti?

A small, fixed-dose oil enema considered gentle enough for wider and more regular use, under a doctor's guidance.

Will a same-gender staff member give the basti?

Yes. Basti is given privately by trained staff of the same gender, with your dignity respected throughout.
