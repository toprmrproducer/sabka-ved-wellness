Nadi Shodhana pranayama in Patna

Nadi shodhana pranayama is alternate nostril breathing, widely practised in India as anulom vilom. One nostril is gently closed while breathing through the other, alternating in a steady rhythm. After Panchakarma it is used as a calm daily practice to support relaxation, sleep and breath awareness, and research suggests it may help lower blood pressure, though studies vary in quality.

Walk through any Patna park at six in the morning, from Eco Park to the lawns near the Ganga, and you will see people sitting cross-legged, a thumb on one nostril, breathing slowly. Anulom vilom has become one of India's most familiar health habits, helped along by television yoga programmes and countless family WhatsApp forwards. Its classical name is nadi shodhana, "purification of the channels", and it is one of the simplest and safest practices we suggest at the end of a Panchakarma programme.

What Nadi Shodhana is

Nadi means channel and shodhana means cleansing. In yoga philosophy, nadis are subtle channels through which prana, life energy, is said to flow. Two of the most important are ida, linked with the left nostril, and pingala, linked with the right. Nadi shodhana alternates the breath between the two, with the aim of balancing them.

In practice, you sit comfortably with a straight spine. The right thumb gently closes the right nostril while you breathe in through the left; the ring finger then closes the left nostril while you breathe out through the right; then you breathe in through the right, switch, and breathe out through the left. That is one round. Beginners breathe without holding the breath. Advanced, classical versions add breath retention (kumbhaka) and fixed ratios, but these should be learned only with a teacher and are not suitable for everyone.

Many people use "anulom vilom" and "nadi shodhana" interchangeably. Some teachers reserve nadi shodhana for the version with retention, and anulom vilom for the simpler version. The simple version is what we recommend for most patients.

History: tradition and texts

Breath control has a long history in Indian thought. Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, generally dated to the early centuries of the common era, list pranayama as the fourth limb of yoga. Detailed instructions for alternate nostril breathing appear in the hatha yoga texts, especially the Hatha Yoga Pradipika, usually dated to around the 15th century, which describes the purification of the nadis through alternating breath and claims, in traditional language, that it makes the body light and the mind clear. The Ayurvedic classics, Charaka, Sushruta and Ashtanga Hridaya, discuss prana and its role in health in detail but do not describe this practice as a technique. Its use alongside Ayurveda is a natural modern combination of sister traditions.

How it is understood to work, and what research shows

The traditional view

Ayurveda describes prana vata as the force governing breathing, the mind and the senses. Irregular, shallow, hurried breathing is read as a sign of disturbed vata, often seen in anxiety, poor sleep and digestive upset. Slow, even, alternate breathing is believed to steady prana vata, calm the mind and support every other system, including digestion.

Modern research

Alternate nostril breathing has been studied more than most yoga practices. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in Complementary Medicine Research, covering randomised trials, found that alternate nostril breathing was associated with reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared with control groups. The authors cautioned that heterogeneity between studies was high and that most studies were not double-blinded, so the results should be interpreted carefully. A 2017 study by Telles and colleagues in Medical Science Monitor Basic Research reported reduced blood pressure and better performance in a vigilance test after alternate nostril breathing in a single session, and a 2024 randomised crossover study in the International Journal of Yoga examined oxygen consumption during nostril breathing practices. Overall, the evidence is encouraging for relaxation and blood pressure, but it is not strong enough to replace blood pressure medicine, and it does not show that nadi shodhana cures any disease.

Who may benefit

  • People recovering from Panchakarma who want a calm daily routine
  • Those with stress, anxiety and racing thoughts, alongside appropriate care
  • People with trouble winding down at night
  • Those with mildly raised blood pressure, alongside their physician's treatment
  • Anyone who wants to build breath awareness before meditation

Who should take care

  • Breath retention (kumbhaka) should be avoided in pregnancy, heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma, epilepsy and after recent surgery; the simple version without retention is usually suitable, but check first
  • Blocked nose, deviated septum or nasal polyps may make it uncomfortable; do not force breath through a blocked nostril
  • Stop if you feel dizzy, breathless, light-headed or anxious, and breathe normally
  • Asthma or COPD: gentle versions may be possible, but discuss with your doctor and keep your inhaler nearby
  • Severe anxiety, panic attacks or psychiatric conditions: start gently and keep your treating doctor informed

Never stop blood pressure, heart, diabetes or psychiatric medicines because you have started pranayama. Any change should be decided with your doctor, based on measured readings.

Learning it at Sabka Ved Wellness

When to begin

During the cleansing days and the graded diet, gentle, natural breathing and rest are enough. Once you have returned to normal meals and the doctor confirms you are ready, nadi shodhana can begin as part of your recovery routine, often alongside the gentle yoga postures described on our yoga page.

How it is taught, for understanding

  1. Sit comfortably on the floor or a chair, spine upright, shoulders relaxed, ideally on an empty stomach in the morning or evening.
  2. Rest the left hand on the knee. Fold the index and middle fingers of the right hand, using the thumb and ring finger to close the nostrils.
  3. Close the right nostril and breathe in slowly through the left.
  4. Close the left nostril, open the right and breathe out slowly through the right.
  5. Breathe in through the right, close it, and breathe out through the left. This is one round.
  6. Begin with 5 to 10 rounds, keeping the breath soft, silent and comfortable. Build up gradually to 10 or 15 minutes.
  7. Finish with a minute of normal breathing with eyes closed.

Dr. Aditya Kunal will check your posture and rhythm so you can practise confidently at home. If your blood pressure is being treated, he may suggest keeping a simple log of readings to share with your physician.

Making it a habit

Link it to something you already do: after brushing your teeth, after pratimarsha nasya, or before dinner. Five minutes every day is more useful than an hour once a week. Gut and metabolic health, Dr. Kunal's focus, benefits from regularity of every kind: meals, sleep and breath.

Common mistakes

  • Breathing forcefully or noisily, which defeats the purpose
  • Practising immediately after a meal
  • Holding the breath without guidance
  • Hunching the shoulders or bending the neck
  • Expecting dramatic results in days rather than steady change over weeks
  • Practising in smoky or dusty air; on bad air days in winter, practise indoors with windows closed
  • Skipping it on busy days; even two or three minutes keeps the habit alive

Why it costs what it does

Nadi shodhana itself costs nothing once learned. The guidance at the end of a Panchakarma programme is part of the consultation and follow-up. Its value is in learning it correctly, safely and in a form that suits your health.

FAQ

Questions about Nadi Shodhana pranayama

Is Nadi Shodhana the same as Anulom Vilom?

They are often used interchangeably for alternate nostril breathing. Some teachers use nadi shodhana for the version with breath retention and anulom vilom for the simpler version.

Can Nadi Shodhana lower blood pressure?

A 2024 meta-analysis found reductions in blood pressure, but study quality varied. It may support blood pressure care but should never replace prescribed medicine.

When should I start pranayama after Panchakarma?

After the graded diet is complete and normal meals return, once the doctor confirms you are ready.

Who should avoid breath holding?

People who are pregnant or have heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, glaucoma, epilepsy or recent surgery should avoid breath retention.

How long should I practise?

Start with 5 to 10 rounds and build up to about 10 or 15 minutes daily. Comfort matters more than duration.
