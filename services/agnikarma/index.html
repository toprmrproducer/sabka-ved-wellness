Agnikarma in Patna

Agnikarma is a classical parasurgical procedure from Sushruta Samhita in which a qualified doctor applies controlled heat to precise points on the skin with a heated metal rod (shalaka). It is used mainly for chronic, localised musculoskeletal pain such as heel, knee or shoulder pain. It is always doctor-performed, after careful screening, and needs proper wound care afterwards.

Most people are surprised to learn that one of Ayurveda's oldest pain treatments involves fire. Agnikarma, literally "action of fire", is a procedure in which a doctor touches heated metal to carefully chosen points over a painful area, creating small, controlled superficial burns. It sounds alarming, and it should never be casual. But it has a long, well-documented history in Indian surgery, it is taught in Shalya Tantra (Ayurvedic surgery) departments across India today, and in properly selected patients with stubborn local pain it remains in active clinical use.

This page explains it plainly so that you can understand what it involves, what is and is not known, and why it must only ever be done by a qualified doctor.

What Agnikarma is

In Agnikarma, an instrument called a shalaka, a metal rod traditionally made of gold, silver, copper, iron or an alloy of five metals (panchadhatu), is heated and then applied briefly to the skin at points over the area of pain. The result is a pattern of tiny, superficial burn marks, dots, rings or lines, in the outermost layers of skin. Other heating materials described in the texts include heated ghee, honey, jaggery, oil and certain stones, chosen according to the depth of tissue being targeted.

A long, documented surgical history

Agnikarma is described in detail in Sushruta Samhita, the foundational text of Indian surgery, whose core is generally dated to the centuries around the start of the common era. Sushruta devotes a chapter of the Sutrasthana to agnikarma vidhi, the method of fire therapy. He describes instruments and materials, the shapes of the marks, the tissues targeted, the signs of a correct burn (samyak dagdha) and the signs of over- and under-burning, and the conditions for which it is suited. He also lists the people in whom it should be avoided. In traditional language, Sushruta praises agnikarma as superior to kshara (caustic alkali) and says that diseases treated by it do not recur. That claim is part of the tradition and should be read as such, not as a modern clinical promise.

Charaka and Vagbhata also refer to agnikarma within the management of particular conditions. In the 20th and 21st centuries, Ayurvedic surgery departments in India have revived and standardised the method, with modern sterilisation, controlled heat sources and wound care.

How it is understood to work, and what research shows

The classical view

Fire is hot, sharp, light and penetrating. Vata and kapha, the doshas most often blamed for chronic pain and stiffness, are cold. The texts reason that applying controlled heat to the site counters the cold, obstructive qualities of vata and kapha, opens the channels and relieves pain. The depth of the burn is chosen according to whether the problem is in the skin, muscle, ligaments, joints or bone.

What modern research shows

The research base is growing but still limited. A 2022 open-labelled clinical trial in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine studied a protocol of parasurgical procedures, including agnikarma, in 100 patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain and reported significant reductions in pain scores from baseline. The trial had no control group, which limits what it can prove. Case reports in the same journal describe agnikarma for an acute migraine episode (2024) and for De Quervain's tenosynovitis using a gold shalaka (2026). Researchers have proposed explanations involving local heat effects on circulation, inflammation and pain signalling, but these remain hypotheses. In short: promising clinical experience and early studies, but not yet the level of controlled evidence that would justify bold claims.

Who may be considered

After examination and investigations where needed, the doctor may consider Agnikarma for chronic, localised pain such as:

  • Heel pain, including plantar fasciitis and heel spur pain
  • Chronic knee pain from osteoarthritis, as part of a wider plan
  • Tennis elbow, frozen shoulder after the acute phase, and similar localised tendon and joint pain
  • Some chronic low back and neck pain with localised tender points
  • Selected skin growths such as warts and corns, in the surgical tradition

Who must not have Agnikarma

  • People with bleeding disorders or on blood thinners, unless the treating physician and Ayurvedic surgeon have reviewed the risk
  • Uncontrolled diabetes, poor circulation or peripheral neuropathy, where burns heal poorly and infection risk is higher
  • Pregnancy
  • Very young children, the very elderly and the very weak, as Sushruta himself cautioned
  • Those with a strong pitta constitution or current heat disorders, by classical teaching, which the doctor will assess
  • Skin infection, eczema, psoriasis or broken skin at the site
  • Tendency to keloids or abnormal scarring
  • Pain from fracture, infection, tumour or nerve compression with weakness, which needs other care

Agnikarma must never be done at home, by untrained people or with improvised heated objects. Doing so can cause serious burns, infection and scarring.

What happens at Sabka Ved Wellness

Consultation and screening

Agnikarma is considered only after a full consultation with Dr. Aditya Kunal. He examines the painful area, reviews X-rays or other reports, checks blood sugar status and medicines, and explains the procedure, the expected marks, the healing time and the alternatives. If Agnikarma is not appropriate, or another therapy is more suitable, you are told so. You decide only after you understand what is involved, and written consent is taken.

The procedure, described for understanding

  1. The most tender points are identified and marked.
  2. The area is cleaned. A cooling application such as aloe vera pulp may be kept ready.
  3. The shalaka is heated to the required temperature.
  4. The doctor touches the heated tip to each marked point for a fraction of a second, producing a small superficial mark in a planned pattern.
  5. The area is soothed with aloe vera, or with ghee and honey as classically described, and covered if needed.

Most patients describe a sharp, brief sting at each point. The whole application is usually over in a few minutes.

Aftercare

The marks are kept clean and dry and should not be scratched or picked. Small blisters or scabs form and usually heal over a week or two, leaving light marks that often fade over time. You will be told how to care for them, when to return, and which warning signs need attention, such as spreading redness, pus, fever or increasing pain. Avoid soaking the area in water until advised. Eat light, warm, easily digested food, and avoid very spicy and sour foods during healing.

Why it costs what it does

The procedure itself is brief, but the cost reflects what makes it safe: the doctor's time for examination, explanation and the procedure itself, sterilised instruments, a controlled heat source, dressings and soothing applications, and follow-up visits to check healing. Agnikarma is never delegated to a therapist. Fixed prices are not published, because plans vary, but what is included is explained beforehand.

FAQ

Questions about Agnikarma

What is Agnikarma?

Agnikarma is a classical Ayurvedic parasurgical procedure, described by Sushruta, in which a doctor applies controlled heat with a heated metal rod to precise points over a painful area.

Is Agnikarma painful?

Most patients feel a sharp, brief sting at each point lasting a fraction of a second. The area is soothed immediately afterwards.

Does Agnikarma leave marks?

Yes, small superficial marks that scab and heal over one to two weeks, usually fading over time. People prone to keloids should not have it.

Is Agnikarma proven for heel pain?

Early studies and case reports are encouraging, including a 2022 uncontrolled trial in chronic musculoskeletal pain, but controlled evidence is still limited. It is not a guaranteed cure.

Who should not have Agnikarma?

People with bleeding disorders or on blood thinners, uncontrolled diabetes, neuropathy, pregnancy, skin disease at the site, keloid tendency, and the very young, old or weak should not have it without careful review.

Can a therapist do Agnikarma?

No. At Sabka Ved Wellness Agnikarma is performed only by the doctor, after screening and consent.
