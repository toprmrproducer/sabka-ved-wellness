सिरावेध venesection

Siravedha in Patna

Siravedha, also called siravyadha, is the puncture of a selected vein under sterile conditions to let out a limited, measured amount of blood for specific conditions. Sushruta regarded it as half of all surgical treatment. It is performed only by a qualified doctor, only after blood tests and careful selection, and is unsuitable for many people.

Sushruta made a striking claim about siravedha. In the same way that basti is called half of all treatment in the Charaka tradition, he said venesection is half of all surgical therapy, and some say the whole of it. That tells us how central the procedure was in classical surgical Ayurveda. It also tells us why it was surrounded with so many rules: a procedure that powerful had to be used carefully.

What siravedha means

Sira (सिरा) means vein or blood vessel, and vedha or vyadha (वेध, व्यध) means piercing. Siravedha is venesection: piercing a vein to release blood. It is one of the instrument-based (shastra) methods of raktamokshana.

Textual history

The Sushruta Samhita devotes a chapter of the Sharira Sthana to siravyadha, describing which veins may be pierced and which must never be, the instruments, the method of applying a tourniquet, the position of the patient for different sites, the amount of blood to be let, the signs of proper and improper venesection, and the management of complications. It lists specific sites for specific conditions, for example sites near the ankle and knee in certain leg conditions and sciatica-like pain, and sites on the forearm for others. Sushruta describes venesection mainly for strong adults in conditions where blood is considered vitiated over a wider area than leeches can address.

Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya also describes siravyadha. In modern India, Ayurvedic surgery (Shalya Tantra) and Panchakarma departments continue to perform it with sterile disposable needles in place of classical instruments, and published case series from teaching hospitals document its use.

How it is understood to work

Classical theory

When rakta is vitiated by pitta, or when blood and vata together obstruct a region, the texts describe symptoms such as burning, redness, swelling, heaviness and particular patterns of pain. Removing blood from a vein linked to the affected region is thought to relieve the congestion and its symptoms faster than oral medicines can. The doctor examines the colour, flow and clotting of the blood as it comes out, which the texts use as indicators of which dosha is involved and when to stop.

Evidence today

Siravedha has been studied mainly in small, single-centre studies in Ayurvedic journals, often for sciatica (gridhrasi), heel pain, varicose vein symptoms and certain skin conditions. A 2022 open-label trial in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine included venesection within a protocol for chronic musculoskeletal pain and reported improvement without adverse events, but there was no control group, and venesection was combined with other methods. There is no large controlled trial establishing siravedha for any condition. Modern medicine does use therapeutic phlebotomy for specific diseases such as haemochromatosis and polycythaemia vera, which should be managed by specialist physicians.

Who may be considered

  • Selected strong adults with conditions the doctor judges to involve rakta, such as certain painful conditions of the legs and some skin presentations.
  • Patients whose blood counts, clotting and general health have been checked and found adequate.
  • Those who have understood the procedure, its alternatives and its risks.

Who must not have siravedha

  • Anaemia, low haemoglobin, low platelets or any bleeding disorder.
  • People on anticoagulants or antiplatelet medicines, unless the prescribing doctor and ours agree, which is rarely appropriate.
  • Children, elderly people, and anyone weak, emaciated or recovering from illness.
  • Pregnancy, the postpartum period and during menstruation.
  • Heart disease, heart failure, low blood pressure, a history of fainting.
  • Generalised oedema, liver cirrhosis, kidney disease.
  • Uncontrolled diabetes or immune suppression, because of infection and healing risks.
  • Immediately after vamana, virechana or basti.
  • Extreme hot or cold weather, as classically advised.

Never stop a prescribed medicine to make yourself eligible for siravedha. Discuss any medicines with your prescribing doctor and with Dr. Aditya Kunal.

What happens at Sabka Ved Wellness

Selection

Dr. Aditya Kunal examines you, decides whether venesection is appropriate, chooses the site and the planned volume, and requests recent blood tests. Many patients are advised other approaches instead. A written explanation of the procedure and your consent are taken before proceeding.

The procedure, described for understanding

  1. A light, suitable meal is taken beforehand, as the texts advise, and sometimes mild oleation and sudation of the region are given.
  2. You are positioned according to the site. A tourniquet is applied above the chosen vein to make it prominent.
  3. The skin is cleaned with antiseptic. The doctor punctures the vein with a sterile, single-use needle.
  4. Blood is allowed to flow into a measured container. The doctor watches the colour and flow and your pulse, breathing and comfort continuously.
  5. When the planned amount has been let, or earlier if the doctor sees signs to stop, the tourniquet is released, the needle removed and firm pressure applied.
  6. A sterile dressing is placed. You lie down and rest, sip water and are observed until stable.

Aftercare

Keep the dressing clean and dry and avoid heavy lifting, exercise, long standing, hot baths and alcohol for the advised period. Eat a light, nourishing diet and drink adequate water. Report any rebleeding, swelling, spreading redness, fever, dizziness or palpitations straight away.

Questions to ask any clinic offering venesection

Because siravedha is invasive, it is reasonable to ask questions before agreeing to it, wherever you have it done. We encourage patients to ask:

  • Who will perform the procedure, and are they a qualified doctor?
  • Have my blood counts and, where relevant, clotting been checked?
  • Why is venesection chosen for me rather than leech therapy, cupping or oral treatment?
  • How much blood is planned to be let, and how will you decide when to stop?
  • Are needles and instruments sterile and single use?
  • What should I do, and whom should I call, if bleeding or swelling starts at home?

A good clinic will answer these openly. If the answers are vague, that is a reason to pause.

How siravedha fits into a wider plan

Venesection is rarely a stand-alone event. Classical practice places it within a plan that may include oral medicines, diet changes that reduce pitta and heat, and other external therapies, and it is not repeated frequently. In conditions such as chronic leg pain or skin disease, the doctor will usually try gentler measures first and consider venesection only if they are insufficient and the patient is strong enough. Follow-up after the procedure matters as much as the procedure itself, so that the response can be judged and the plan adjusted.

Complications the texts and modern practice both recognise

The classical texts describe complications of improper venesection, including excessive bleeding, fainting, injury to deeper structures and failure to bleed. Modern practice adds infection, bruising and nerve irritation. These are uncommon in careful hands but real, which is why we insist on selection, sterility and observation.

Where the cost comes from

Siravedha requires a doctor's undivided time, sterile disposables, pre-procedure blood tests, a private procedure space, dressing materials and supervised recovery. It is usually a single, carefully prepared sitting rather than a package. We do not publish prices online; if it is appropriate for you, the plan and its costs are explained at consultation.

Who should be thinking about this

Siravedha is not a cure for sciatica, varicose veins, skin disease or anything else, and most people are better served by other treatments. For a small group of carefully selected patients, it is a classical option with a long surgical history. If you would like an honest opinion, request an appointment or book a consultation with Dr. Aditya Kunal online, and bring your recent blood reports.

FAQ

Questions about Siravedha

How is siravedha different from leech therapy?

Siravedha is a direct puncture of a selected vein with a sterile needle and is usually reserved for strong adults with wider involvement. Leech therapy draws a smaller amount from a local site and is classically preferred for more delicate patients and localised conditions.

How much blood is let out?

A limited amount decided by the doctor based on your strength, the condition and the site. It is planned in advance and monitored throughout.

Is siravedha the same as donating blood?

No. Blood donation is collected for others from healthy donors. Siravedha is a classical therapeutic procedure for selected conditions, with its own sites, rules and aftercare.

Will I feel weak afterwards?

Some people feel tired for the day. You rest and are observed before leaving. Dizziness, palpitations or rebleeding should be reported immediately.

Can siravedha be done if I am on aspirin?

Usually not. Antiplatelet and anticoagulant medicines raise bleeding risk. Do not stop them yourself; discuss with your prescribing doctor.
