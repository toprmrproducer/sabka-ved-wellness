Pichu for back, joints and neck in Patna

Pichu for the back, joints and neck is a simple Ayurvedic therapy in which a thick pad soaked in warm medicated oil is placed over the painful area, such as the lower back (kati pichu), knee (janu pichu) or neck (greeva pichu), and kept warm for a set time. It is used as a gentler, more practical alternative to local basti for vata-type pain and stiffness.

Kati basti, the well of warm oil built on the lower back with a ring of dough, is one of the best-known Ayurvedic therapies for back pain. It is also demanding: you must lie face down without moving for 30 to 45 minutes, and the dough wall can leak if you shift. For many patients, especially the elderly, people with breathing difficulty or those who simply cannot lie prone for long, there is a quieter alternative. A thick cotton pad soaked in the same warm oil is laid over the painful area and kept warm. That is pichu.

What Pichu means, and its regional names

Pichu is the Sanskrit word for a pad or swab of cotton. The therapy is named by the area treated:

  • Kati pichu: on the lower back (kati)
  • Janu pichu: on the knee (janu)
  • Greeva pichu: on the neck (greeva)
  • Prishtha pichu: on the mid or upper back
  • Amsa pichu: on the shoulder

Shiro pichu, on the head, and yoni pichu, in gynaecology, use the same principle but have their own pages because their purposes are different.

Origins

Pichu as a method of applying oil or medicine appears throughout the classical texts. Sushruta Samhita uses pichu frequently in wound care, gynaecology and local treatments, and Ashtanga Hridaya describes shiro pichu among the head oil therapies. The specific forms kati pichu, janu pichu and greeva pichu as practised in clinics today are best described as practical extensions of this classical method to musculoskeletal pain, developed in modern clinical Ayurveda and Kerala practice. We have not found them named individually in the core classics, so we describe them honestly as classical technique applied to new sites, rather than as named classical procedures.

How it is understood to work

The classical view

Vata, the dosha of movement, is cold, dry, light and rough. When it accumulates in the joints and spine, the texts describe pain, stiffness, cracking sounds and restricted movement, typically worse in cold weather and early morning and better with warmth and oil. Snehana (oleation) with warm medicated oil counters these qualities. Pichu keeps the oil in contact with the area for an extended time, supposedly allowing deeper action than a brief massage, while the warmth relaxes the muscles.

What research shows

Specific research on kati, janu or greeva pichu is very limited. Published Ayurvedic reports, such as a 2025 case report on acute intervertebral disc prolapse and a 2013 report on multimodal management of knee osteoarthritis, describe local oil therapies as part of combined plans with internal medicines, diet and other procedures. These cannot tell us how much the pichu alone contributed. Local warmth is widely accepted as comforting for muscle-related pain, and medicated oils are a long-standing tradition. We present pichu as a supportive therapy for comfort and mobility, not as a treatment that repairs discs, cartilage or nerves.

Who may benefit

  • Chronic low back pain and stiffness, after red-flag causes have been excluded
  • Neck stiffness from posture, long screen hours or two-wheeler riding
  • Knee stiffness and pain in older adults, as one part of a wider plan
  • Shoulder stiffness, including after the acute phase of a frozen shoulder
  • People who cannot tolerate kati basti or janu basti positioning

Who should avoid it or wait

  • Hot, red, swollen joints, or joint pain with fever, which need medical evaluation
  • Recent injury or sprain in the first few days
  • Skin infection, eczema, open wounds or rash over the area
  • Back pain with leg weakness, numbness in the groin area, or loss of bladder or bowel control: this is an emergency
  • Back or neck pain after a fall or accident until fracture is ruled out
  • Unexplained weight loss, night pain or a history of cancer with new back pain, until evaluated
  • Indigestion, heaviness and swelling with ama, where dry therapies may come first

Pichu does not replace physiotherapy, orthopaedic care or surgery when needed. Continue any medicines your doctor has prescribed.

Assessment

Dr. Aditya Kunal takes a careful history: when the pain began, whether it radiates, what worsens it, and any reports such as X-rays or MRI. He also asks about digestion and bowel habits. In Ayurveda, chronic constipation and gas are closely tied to vata in the lower back, and correcting them is often part of treating back pain. He then decides whether pichu, local basti, upanaha, internal medicines, or a combination suits you.

Preparation

The chosen oil, often a classical vata-calming preparation, is warmed in a water bath. A thick, clean pad of cotton or layered cloth is prepared for you.

The session, for understanding

  1. You lie or sit in a comfortable position, depending on the area. Many patients can sit for neck or knee pichu.
  2. The area may be massaged lightly with warm oil first.
  3. The pad is soaked in warm oil, squeezed to stop dripping and placed over the area.
  4. It may be covered with a cloth to hold warmth, and re-soaked in warm oil or replaced as it cools.
  5. After 30 to 45 minutes it is removed, the area is massaged gently and sometimes given brief local steam.

This explains the procedure; it is not a home instruction, since oil temperature and choice matter.

Aftercare and diet

Keep the area warm and covered. Avoid cold baths, cold drinks, heavy lifting and long two-wheeler rides on treatment days. Gentle movement and any exercises suggested by the doctor or physiotherapist are encouraged. Eat warm, freshly cooked food, and include a little ghee if your digestion handles it. Avoid stale food, excess curd and chana-heavy dishes that cause gas, as the texts associate them with aggravated vata.

Why it costs what it costs

Pichu uses a generous quantity of classical medicated oil, which must be kept warm and replenished during the session, along with a fresh pad and linen for every patient and 30 to 45 minutes of therapist attention. It generally uses less oil and preparation than kati basti, which is one practical advantage. The doctor's assessment decides whether it is the right choice for your pain. A note on everyday habits

Patna's roads, long commutes on two-wheelers, low sofas and hours bent over phones all load the neck and lower back. Small changes help any therapy: a chair that supports the lower back, a screen at eye level, breaks every 45 minutes, and a pillow that keeps the neck straight. The doctor may also suggest gentle yoga once the acute pain settles. Keep sleeping on a firm but not hard mattress, avoid lifting heavy buckets or gas cylinders with a bent back, and in winter keep the neck and lower back covered on early morning rides. These habits sound ordinary, but Ayurveda counts them as part of the treatment, because they remove the daily triggers that keep vata disturbed in the spine and joints.

FAQ

Questions about Pichu for back, joints and neck

What is Kati Pichu?

Kati pichu is an Ayurvedic therapy in which a thick pad soaked in warm medicated oil is placed on the lower back for 30 to 45 minutes to ease pain and stiffness.

Is Pichu as good as Kati Basti?

They suit different patients. Kati basti holds more oil in a dough ring and needs you to lie still face down. Pichu is gentler and more flexible, and is often preferred for the elderly or those who cannot lie prone.

Can Pichu fix a slipped disc?

No therapy can honestly promise that. Pichu may ease pain and stiffness as part of a wider plan, but disc problems need proper evaluation, and some need specialist care.

When should I not have Pichu for back pain?

Avoid it and seek urgent care if you have leg weakness, numbness in the groin, or loss of bladder or bowel control. Also avoid it with fever, fresh injury, skin infection or hot, swollen joints.

How many sessions are usually needed?

Commonly 7 to 14 sessions, decided after assessment.
