सेक / परिषेक medicated liquid sprinkling

Sekam / Parisheka in Patna

Sekam, known in Sanskrit as seka or parisheka, is the Ayurvedic practice of pouring or sprinkling a warm or cool medicated liquid, such as herbal decoction, milk or oil, over the whole body, a painful area, a wound or the closed eyes. It is used for pain and stiffness, local swelling, wound care and some eye complaints, depending on the liquid chosen.

Sekam is one of those therapies that looks deceptively ordinary. Warm liquid is poured, steadily and from a set height, over a body part. There is no elaborate equipment, no special table and no complicated preparation for the patient. And yet the classical texts give it a clear place in three different fields of Ayurveda: as a form of sweating therapy for pain and stiffness, as a soothing wash for wounds and inflamed skin, and as one of the kriyakalpa procedures for the eyes. What changes between them is the liquid, its temperature and the area.

What Sekam and Parisheka mean

Seka and parisheka both come from Sanskrit roots meaning to sprinkle, pour or bathe. Sekam is the form commonly used in Kerala and South Indian practice. In the clinic the word covers:

  • Sarvanga parisheka: warm liquid poured over the whole body, a form of drava sweda (sudation by liquids). When oil is used and two therapists massage while pouring, it becomes pizhichil, which has its own page.
  • Ekanga sekam: pouring over one area, such as a painful knee, shoulder or the lower back.
  • Vrana seka: pouring a cleansing decoction over a wound or inflamed skin.
  • Netra seka or parisheka: a thin stream of medicated liquid poured over the closed eyes.

Classical history

Charaka Samhita lists parisheka among the thirteen forms of sweating therapy that use heat, describing the pouring of warm decoctions, milk, oil or meat soups over the body for vata disorders. Sushruta and Vagbhata include it within drava sweda. Sushruta, the classic of surgery, describes seka for wounds, swellings and inflammation, often with cooling decoctions. In eye care, Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya (around 7th century CE) describes seka as one of the kriyakalpa procedures, along with tarpana, putapaka, aschyotana and anjana, specifying the height of the stream and the duration by dosha.

So Sekam is firmly classical. The Kerala tradition has refined techniques for local and whole-body pouring, and dhara therapies such as dhanyamla dhara and kashaya dhara are, in effect, specialised forms of seka.

How it is understood to work

Classical reasoning

The principle is contact and temperature. A warm stream over a cold, stiff joint brings heat and softness and is used for vata. A cool stream of milk-based or cooling decoction over hot, inflamed skin or burning eyes is used for pitta. A warm, sharp decoction, sometimes with fermented liquids, may be used over heavy, swollen areas for kapha. The continuous flow maintains a steady temperature and keeps the medicine in fresh contact with the tissue.

What research shows

The best documented modern use is in eye care. A 2014 randomised comparative study in Ayu from the National Institute of Ayurveda, Jaipur, compared keshanjana and netra parisheka with artificial tear drops in 32 patients with dry eye syndrome (shushkakshipaka). Studies like this are small but show that the method has been formally tested. In wound care, a 2025 case report described integrative management of a diabetic foot ulcer that included local Ayurvedic washes, and a 2020 case report on Guillain-Barre syndrome described a multi-therapy plan that included sudation procedures. These reports involve combinations of treatments, so the effect of sekam alone cannot be separated. We describe it as a useful traditional technique with limited, early clinical evidence.

Who may benefit

  • Joint and muscle stiffness, pain and cramps (warm sekam)
  • Swelling in joints, especially with kapha signs (dhanyamla or kashaya sekam)
  • Inflamed or itchy skin, as part of a dermatology plan (cooling decoctions)
  • Burning, tired or dry eyes, after an eye examination (netra seka)
  • Wound care support, alongside proper medical dressing and, where needed, surgical care
  • Patients too weak for heavier heat therapies

Contraindications and cautions

  • Fever and acute infection, for whole-body warm sekam
  • Heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure or fainting with heat, for warm whole-body pouring
  • Open, deep or infected wounds should be treated with sterile, prescribed solutions only, under close supervision
  • Eye redness with pain, sudden blurred vision, eye injury or eye infection need urgent eye specialist care, not seka
  • Diabetic neuropathy, where temperature must be checked carefully
  • Pregnancy, for warm whole-body procedures, unless specifically advised

Sekam does not replace antibiotics, wound debridement, eye treatment or any medicine your doctor has prescribed.

Sekam at Sabka Ved Wellness

Assessment

Dr. Aditya Kunal first decides which kind of sekam, if any, suits you: warm or cool, decoction, milk, oil or a fermented liquid, whole body or one area. For eye or wound concerns he examines the area carefully and refers you onward if specialist care is needed.

Preparation

Decoctions are boiled fresh from herbs and strained; milk preparations are made the same day; oils are warmed in a water bath. Temperature is checked before every pour. All liquid used on you is used for you alone.

The procedure, for understanding

  1. You lie or sit comfortably, with the area exposed and the rest of the body covered.
  2. The liquid is poured in a steady stream from a vessel, at a height and speed suited to the area. For the eyes, the stream is thin and the eyes are closed.
  3. Pouring continues for the set time, while the liquid is kept at a constant temperature.
  4. The area is then wiped dry and kept covered and warm, or rested if a cooling sekam was used.

Please do not attempt eye or wound washes at home without a prescription; the wrong liquid or poor hygiene can cause harm.

Aftercare and diet

After warm sekam, avoid cold water, fans and cold drinks for a while. After cooling sekam for heat, avoid sun exposure and very spicy food. Diet follows the dosha being treated: warm, light meals for vata and kapha; cooling, simple foods such as rice, moong dal, lauki and buttermilk at midday for pitta.

Choosing the liquid: a doctor's decision

The same pouring technique can soothe or aggravate depending on what is poured. A common example: someone with a hot, swollen knee in the humid weeks of September may be made worse by warm oil poured over it, because heat and oil add to the inflammation and heaviness. For that knee, a warm, light decoction or a fermented liquid such as dhanyamla, which the texts describe as cutting through kapha and swelling, may be chosen instead. Another person with a cold, cracking, dry knee in January may need just the opposite, a nourishing warm oil. Milk-based preparations are kept for heat and burning, and are avoided where swelling and heaviness dominate.

This is why sekam is planned after examination and why the liquid can change during a course as the condition changes. It is also why we discourage copying a neighbour's home treatment for a joint that only looks similar.

Why it costs what it does

Sekam uses litres of freshly prepared decoction, milk or oil, depending on the area, which must be kept at a constant temperature throughout. Therapist attention is continuous, linen is single-patient, and vessels must be cleaned thoroughly between uses. Eye procedures need additional care with hygiene. Doctor oversight decides the liquid and duration. Prices are not published, because plans vary, but what is included is explained before you start.

FAQ

Questions about Sekam / Parisheka

What is Sekam in Ayurveda?

Sekam, or parisheka, is the pouring or sprinkling of a warm or cool medicated liquid over the body, a specific area, a wound or the closed eyes.

Is Sekam the same as Pizhichil?

Pizhichil is a whole-body form of sekam that uses warm oil with simultaneous massage by two therapists. Sekam is the broader term and can use decoctions or milk on one area.

Can Sekam be done on the eyes?

Yes, netra seka is a classical eye procedure, but only after an eye examination. Eye pain, redness with blurred vision or injury needs an eye specialist first.

Is there research on Sekam?

A small 2014 randomised study tested netra parisheka in dry eye syndrome. Other reports use sekam within combined plans. Overall evidence is limited.

How long is a session?

From about 10 minutes for the eyes to 45 minutes for larger areas, as advised.
