Pizhichil / Sarvanga Dhara / Parisheka in Patna

Pizhichil is a Kerala form of parisheka sweda in which lukewarm medicated oil is squeezed from cloth or poured from vessels in a continuous stream over the whole body while therapists massage gently. It combines oleation and sudation in one sitting and is mainly used for dryness, stiffness and weakness of vata type, after a doctor confirms suitability.

Kerala physicians sometimes call pizhichil a royal treatment, and the nickname is less about luxury than about scale. It needs several therapists working at once, a specially shaped wooden table, and litres of warm medicated oil kept at a steady temperature for close to an hour. There are very few therapies in Ayurveda that demand that much of a clinic, and very few that patients describe as so profoundly calming.

What pizhichil is

Pizhichil is Malayalam for squeezing, which describes how the oil was traditionally applied: a cloth soaked in warm oil is squeezed so that the oil runs in a thin stream onto the body from a small height. The Sanskrit terms are sarvanga dhara (सर्वाङ्ग धारा, a stream over the whole body), kayaseka (sprinkling over the body) and taila parisheka (oil pouring). Classically it belongs to parisheka sweda, a form of sudation by pouring warm liquids, so it oleates and warms at the same time.

History: classical root, Kerala flowering

The Charaka Samhita lists parisheka among the thirteen fire-based forms of sudation in the Swedadhyaya chapter: pouring warm decoctions, milk, oils or meat broths over the body from vessels with small openings. The Ashtanga Hridaya and Sushruta Samhita describe seka and parisheka as well.

Pizhichil as a distinct, named, multi-therapist procedure is a Kerala development. Practitioners trace it to the ashtavaidya physician families and to royal households where it was given to rulers and their families. The method is well documented in Kerala practice and in modern standard operating procedures used by teaching institutions. Romantic stories about specific ancient kings are part of local tradition and cannot be verified.

How it is understood to work

Classical logic

Vata dosha is dry, light, cold and mobile. Pizhichil applies the exact opposite in quantity: warm, heavy, oily, steady. The continuous flow is thought to keep the oil at a constant gentle warmth so that it penetrates without overheating, while the light massage helps absorption. The texts value it in conditions where both oleation and sudation are needed but strong heat would be too drying or exhausting. Oils are selected according to the patient: ksheerabala taila, dhanwantaram taila, bala taila or mahanarayana taila for vata, and cooler preparations where pitta is involved.

What evidence exists

Research on pizhichil itself is sparse. Reports from Kerala hospitals describe its use in neuromuscular and joint conditions, but these are mostly observational or case-based, and pizhichil is usually given alongside other treatments, so its own effect cannot be separated. Research on related oil therapies, such as abhyanga and shirodhara, suggests relaxing and stress-lowering effects in small studies. We describe pizhichil as a classical, deeply oleating and calming therapy, and we do not claim specific disease outcomes for it.

Pizhichil compared with abhyanga and shirodhara

Patients often ask how pizhichil differs from the oil therapies they already know. Abhyanga is a massage with a moderate amount of oil, followed usually by separate steam. Shirodhara pours a thin stream of oil only on the forehead and is aimed mainly at the mind, sleep and the head. Pizhichil pours oil over the whole body continuously, so the body is oleated and warmed at once, without a separate steam session. That is why the classical framework places it among the forms of sudation rather than massage. It is the heaviest of the three in terms of oil and the most demanding in staff, and so it is used selectively, where the doctor judges that the body needs that degree of oleation.

Timing within a programme

Pizhichil can be given as a standalone course for vata conditions, or within a larger plan. When used before cleansing, it is scheduled on preparatory days after digestion is clear. After vamana or virechana, the doctor waits until digestion and strength have recovered before any heavy oil therapy. Summer sessions in Patna may be shorter and scheduled in the early morning, while the cool months of November to February are usually the most comfortable time for a full pizhichil course.

Who may be considered

  • People with marked dryness, stiffness and weakness of vata type, as part of a plan.
  • Some neuromuscular and degenerative conditions, alongside conventional medical care and physiotherapy.
  • Recovery from prolonged illness or exhaustion, where the doctor judges nourishment appropriate.
  • Selected patients in preparation for Panchakarma when both oleation and sudation are needed gently.

Who should not have pizhichil

  • Kapha-dominant states with heaviness, obesity, congestion or significant water retention.
  • Indigestion, coated tongue, poor appetite (ama), or acute fever.
  • Uncontrolled diabetes or high lipids, unless the doctor modifies the plan.
  • Significant heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, or a tendency to faint.
  • Acute infections, open wounds, widespread skin disease or oozing eruptions.
  • Pregnancy.
  • Acute cold, cough or sinus congestion.
  • Very old or frail patients unless specifically cleared.

Tell the doctor about all medicines. Never stop prescribed treatment for an Ayurvedic programme.

How it is done at Sabka Ved Wellness

Before the session

Dr. Aditya Kunal assesses you and decides whether pizhichil is right, which oil, the temperature, duration and number of sessions. The oil is warmed in a vessel kept at a steady, comfortable temperature, which is checked repeatedly through the session.

The procedure, for understanding

  1. You sit on the droni (wooden treatment table) while oil is applied to the head, and a cloth band may be tied around the forehead to stop oil running into the eyes.
  2. You lie down. Two therapists stand on either side, sometimes with a third or fourth to manage the oil.
  3. Each therapist dips a cloth into the warm oil and squeezes it so the oil runs in a steady stream from a small height onto the body, while the other hand gently massages the same area.
  4. Strokes run from the neck down, in seven classical positions (sitting, lying on the back, on each side, on the front, on the back again and sitting), so the whole body is evenly treated.
  5. Oil collecting in the droni is taken out, rewarmed and reused only for the same patient within that session or course.
  6. At the end, excess oil is wiped off, and you rest before a warm bath.

Aftercare

Rest is essential. Avoid cold water, cold air and exertion. Eat a light, warm meal when hungry. Many people sleep deeply after pizhichil. If you feel dizzy, chilled or unusually heavy, tell the staff.

Why pizhichil costs more than almost any external therapy

This is where honesty about cost matters. A single pizhichil session uses several litres of medicated oil, and classical oils are expensive because of their herbs and long preparation. Good practice reserves the oil for one patient only, often for the duration of their course, and then discards it, never pooling it with others. Two to four trained therapists are occupied for most of an hour. The droni, linen and bathing area must be thoroughly cleaned afterwards. The doctor monitors response across the course. Clinics that offer very cheap pizhichil are usually cutting one of these corners: diluted or plain oil, a single therapist, short sessions or shared oil. We do not publish prices online, and the reasons behind your plan's cost are explained openly at consultation.

A balanced view

Pizhichil is a remarkable classical therapy and can be deeply soothing, but it is not a cure for paralysis, arthritis, neurological disease or any other condition, and it is not suitable for everyone. FAQ

Questions about Pizhichil / Sarvanga Dhara / Parisheka

How much oil is used in pizhichil?

Several litres per session, kept warm and reused only for the same patient. The exact volume depends on body size and protocol.

Is pizhichil the same as sarvanga dhara?

They are closely related. Pizhichil is the Kerala name for whole-body oil pouring, usually by squeezing oil-soaked cloth, while sarvanga dhara and taila parisheka are the Sanskrit terms for pouring over the whole body.

Why do several therapists work at once?

To keep the oil flowing continuously and evenly over both sides of the body while massaging, and to manage rewarming of the oil.

Can people with obesity or high cholesterol have pizhichil?

Usually it is not the first choice in heavy, kapha-dominant states. The doctor may suggest dry therapies such as udvartana instead.

Is the oil shared between patients?

No. Oil is reserved for one patient and is not pooled with others.
