स्नेहपान internal oleation with medicated ghee

Snehapana in Patna

Snehapana means drinking measured, gradually increasing doses of medicated ghee (or occasionally oil) on an empty stomach for a few days before vamana or virechana. It is a doctor-supervised preparatory step for people who have been assessed as fit for major cleansing, and it is not suitable for everyone.

Ask anyone who has completed a classical Panchakarma what they remember most, and many will say the ghee. A cup of warm, bitter, medicated ghee at sunrise, a little more each day, followed by hours of waiting for real hunger. It is the least glamorous part of the programme and one of the most important.

Snehapana in plain words

Sneha (स्नेह) means unctuous substance, and also affection. Pana (पान) means drinking. Snehapana is internal oleation: taking medicated fat by mouth so that, in Ayurvedic understanding, it penetrates the tissues, softens accumulated doshas and draws them back toward the digestive tract, from where vamana (therapeutic emesis) or virechana (therapeutic purgation) can remove them.

The fat used is usually a ghee cooked with herbs, such as tiktaka ghrita, mahatiktaka ghrita, panchatikta ghrita, kalyanaka ghrita or plain go-ghrita, chosen according to the person and the condition. Sesame oil and, in classical texts, animal fats are also described.

The texts behind it

The thirteenth chapter of the Sutra Sthana of the Charaka Samhita, called Snehadhyaya, is devoted to oleation. It names four principal fats: ghee, oil, muscle fat and bone marrow, and calls ghee the best because it takes on the qualities of the herbs it is processed with while keeping its own. It discusses dose levels linked to how long a dose takes to digest, the signs of correct, insufficient and excessive oleation, and the complications of misuse. The Sushruta Samhita and Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya repeat and refine these rules.

The later practice of arohana snehapana, starting with a small test dose and stepping it up each day, is described in the classical framework and refined in commentaries and in modern teaching hospitals. Clinicians in Kerala and in the major Ayurveda institutes of Jamnagar, Delhi and Varanasi have standardised many details over the last century. These are documented practices. Claims that a particular family recipe has been handed down unchanged for thousands of years are tradition, not history.

Why ghee, and why gradually?

The dosha logic

Ayurveda describes doshas that have spread into the tissues as stuck, a little like dried residue in a vessel. Oleation softens them; sweating (swedana) then liquefies and mobilises them. Ghee is preferred for pitta and vata conditions and for its ability to carry herbs. The dose is increased each day because the aim is a specific endpoint, not a fixed quantity. Classical signs of adequate oleation (samyak snigdha lakshana) include proper passage of flatus, stronger appetite, softness of the skin, oily or unctuous stools, a feeling of lightness and a slight aversion to more fat. Once these appear, oleation stops.

What research has looked at

Evidence specific to snehapana is limited. A small pilot study published in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine in 2014 explored whether oil enemas in escalating doses could substitute for oral snehapana, noting that many patients find drinking ghee difficult. An experimental study in rats reported in Ayu (2010) examined dietary ghee and serum lipids and found no significant rise in total cholesterol at the doses tested, though triglycerides did rise; animal findings cannot simply be applied to people. A 2016 controlled study in healthy adults following a short Panchakarma-based programme reported changes in some blood metabolites, but the authors themselves suggested diet may have driven much of the effect. Put plainly: snehapana is a classical preparatory method with a long clinical tradition, and it has not been tested in large modern trials.

Because large amounts of ghee are involved, we take lipid status, liver function and blood sugar seriously at assessment, and may ask for recent reports before deciding.

Who may be a candidate

  • People being prepared for vamana or virechana after a full assessment.
  • Some chronic skin, digestive and metabolic concerns where the doctor judges that classical cleansing is appropriate.
  • Adults with adequate strength and digestion, after any ama has been cleared by paachana.

Who should not take snehapana, or only with great caution

  • People with active ama: coated tongue, poor appetite, heaviness (paachana comes first).
  • Uncontrolled diabetes, significant obesity, fatty liver disease or very high lipids, unless the doctor modifies or replaces the method.
  • Gallbladder disease or a history of pancreatitis.
  • Pregnancy.
  • Acute fever, acute diarrhoea, indigestion, or immediately after a heavy meal.
  • Very weak, very young or very elderly people.
  • Anyone with heart, kidney or liver disease who has not been cleared after reviewing their reports.

Never stop prescribed medicines to make room for ghee. Tell the doctor about all medicines, including those for sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol and thyroid.

How it is done at Sabka Ved Wellness

Assessment

Dr. Aditya Kunal reviews your history, examines you, and checks whether your digestion is ready. He selects the ghee, decides the starting dose based on your digestive strength and bowel type (koshtha), and explains what to expect. This is described here so you understand the process; it is not something to attempt at home.

Each morning

  1. You arrive on an empty stomach, usually soon after sunrise.
  2. The measured dose of warm medicated ghee is given, sometimes followed by sips of warm water to help it settle.
  3. You note the time and wait. You drink only warm water when thirsty and do not eat until true hunger returns, which may take several hours.
  4. When hungry, you eat a light, warm meal such as thin rice gruel or khichdi with little fat.
  5. The doctor reviews you daily: appetite, belching, nausea, bowel movements, sleep, energy and the signs of oleation. The next day's dose is set accordingly.

Daily discipline

During these days you avoid cold water, daytime sleep, late nights, exposure to strong wind or sun, heavy exercise, suppressing natural urges and emotional strain. These rules come straight from the classical texts and they make a real difference to comfort.

What comes next

After oleation is complete, there are usually one to three days of abhyanga and swedana, followed by vamana or virechana. Snehapana on its own is not the treatment; it prepares for one.

What it can feel like

Common experiences include mild nausea, loss of appetite, a heavy feeling, loose oily stools and fatigue. These are generally expected and are monitored. Persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, jaundice-like yellowing, breathlessness or chest discomfort are not normal and require immediate medical attention, which is one reason this is always doctor-supervised.

Why the cost reflects more than ghee

A classical medicated ghee is not ordinary ghee. It is cooked for hours with herb decoctions and pastes following set ratios, and good-quality preparations from reliable pharmacies cost far more than kitchen ghee. A course can use a substantial quantity. Add daily doctor review, diet guidance and the abhyanga and swedana days that follow, and you can see why we never offer snehapana as a cheap standalone item. The cost of your programme is explained clearly at consultation; we do not list prices online because each plan differs.

An honest closing word

Snehapana is not a cure for any disease. It is a carefully dosed step that only makes sense inside a properly planned programme. If you are wondering whether you are a candidate, especially if you have diabetes, liver or lipid concerns, or are on regular medicines, the right place to start is a consultation. FAQ

Questions about Snehapana

How much ghee will I have to drink?

There is no fixed amount. The dose starts small and is raised daily according to your digestion and the signs of oleation. The doctor sets each day's dose after reviewing you.

Is snehapana safe if I have high cholesterol or fatty liver?

It may not be. These conditions call for careful assessment and recent reports, and the doctor may modify the method, use an alternative, or advise against it.

Why can't I eat until I feel hungry?

Classical practice holds that the ghee must be digested before food is added. Eating too soon can cause nausea and indigestion. Warm water is allowed in the meantime.

Can I go to work during snehapana?

Many people take these days off or work very lightly. Rest, avoiding cold, late nights and exertion, is part of the method.

Is snehapana enough on its own?

No. It is preparation for vamana or virechana. On its own it is not the cleansing treatment.
