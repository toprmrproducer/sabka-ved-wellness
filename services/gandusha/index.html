गण्डूष holding medicated liquid in the mouth

Gandusha in Patna

Gandusha is a classical Ayurvedic mouth therapy in which the mouth is filled completely with a medicated liquid, such as sesame oil, ghee, milk or decoction, and held without moving it until the eyes and nose begin to water, then spat out. It is used for dryness, gum and tooth strength, mouth ulcers and voice care, as an addition to dental care.

Gandusha asks for something unusual: stillness of the mouth. You fill it completely with warm sesame oil or another medicated liquid, so full that the liquid cannot be swished, and you simply hold it. No gargling, no moving, no talking. After a few minutes your eyes begin to water and your nose begins to run. That, according to the classical texts, is the sign that the procedure is complete. It sounds odd the first time. For many people it becomes a calm, two-minute ritual at the start of the day.

What Gandusha is, and how it differs from Kavala

Gandusha comes from the Sanskrit for holding liquid in the cheeks. The defining feature is quantity: enough liquid to fill the mouth completely, leaving no room for movement. Its sister therapy, kavala, uses a smaller amount that is actively swished and gargled. Modern oil pulling resembles kavala; true gandusha is held still.

Liquids used include sesame oil (the classical favourite), medicated oils, ghee, milk, honey water, meat soup in old texts, herbal decoctions and even cool water for specific purposes, all chosen according to the complaint.

Classical description

Charaka Samhita, in its chapter on daily regimen, recommends gandusha with sesame oil and describes its benefits in traditional language: strength of the jaws, depth of voice, firmness of the face, good taste and relish for food, freedom from dryness of the throat and cracking of the lips, and strong teeth that do not decay or become sensitive and that can chew hard food. Ashtanga Hridaya (around 7th century CE) gives a full chapter to gandusha and kavala, describing four types:

  • Snigdha: with oils or fats, for vata, dryness and sensitivity
  • Shamana: palliative, often cooling, for pitta, burning and ulcers
  • Shodhana: cleansing, with sharp, pungent liquids, for kapha
  • Ropana: healing, with astringent and bitter liquids, for ulcers and wounds

It also describes the method: sitting in a place free of wind, holding the liquid until the mouth fills with secretion and the eyes and nose water, then spitting it out. Gandusha is therefore a fully classical practice that has survived into daily routines in many Indian homes.

How it is understood to work, and the evidence

Classical understanding

Holding a warm, unctuous liquid against the mouth's lining is believed to nourish and strengthen the tissues of the gums, teeth, cheeks and jaw, while drawing out kapha and secretions. The watering of the eyes and nose is read as a sign that the effect has reached the head. Each type of gandusha targets a dosha through the qualities of its liquid.

What research shows

Most modern research has been on oil pulling, which involves swishing rather than holding, so it is closer to kavala than gandusha. A 2009 triple-blind randomised study in the Indian Journal of Dental Research found reductions in plaque and gingivitis scores after sesame oil pulling, comparable to chlorhexidine, in a small group of adolescents. A 2016 systematic review of five small randomised trials concluded that oil pulling may benefit oral hygiene in the short term but that better trials are needed. A 2024 meta-analysis also compared oil pulling with chlorhexidine and other mouthwashes. We are not aware of trials testing classical gandusha specifically. The fair conclusion is that oil-based mouth therapies may support oral hygiene, while specific classical claims about the voice, jaw or senses remain traditional.

Who may benefit

  • Dry mouth and lips, including in older adults and in very dry winter weather
  • Tooth sensitivity and weak gums, alongside dental care
  • Some mouth ulcers, with cooling or healing liquids prescribed for them
  • People who use their voice heavily, such as teachers and singers, as supportive care
  • Those wanting a calm, simple daily oral routine

Who should avoid it

  • Children, who may swallow or choke
  • People with swallowing problems, stroke, severe cough or breathing difficulty
  • Acute cold and nasal blockage, when holding liquid can be uncomfortable
  • Indigestion and nausea, when the texts advise against it
  • Toothache, dental abscess, facial swelling or loose teeth, which need a dentist first
  • Persistent ulcers, white or red patches or a lump in the mouth, which need urgent evaluation

Gandusha does not treat cavities, gum disease or infection. Keep brushing, keep your dental appointments, and continue prescribed treatment.

Consultation

Dr. Aditya Kunal looks at your mouth, tongue and gums, asks about dryness, ulcers, sensitivity and dental history, and also asks about digestion and acidity. Recurrent mouth ulcers, for example, are often linked in Ayurveda with pitta and digestive disturbance, so the liquid for gandusha is chosen alongside diet advice.

How it is done, for understanding

  1. You sit upright in a calm place, away from a fan or wind, ideally in the morning after brushing.
  2. The prescribed liquid, lukewarm if oil, is taken into the mouth until it is full.
  3. You hold it still, without swishing or swallowing, breathing through the nose. The cheeks, neck and forehead may be massaged gently, as the texts describe.
  4. When the mouth fills with secretion and the eyes and nose begin to water, or after the time advised, the liquid is spat out.
  5. The mouth is rinsed with warm water.

If you feel like gagging, use slightly less liquid; comfort is more important than reaching a perfect fill.

Aftercare

Wait a little before eating or drinking. Spit oil into a tissue or bin rather than a drain. Keep up brushing and cleaning between the teeth, and limit sugar and tobacco.

Gandusha for recurrent mouth ulcers

Recurrent small, painful ulcers inside the cheeks and on the tongue are a frequent complaint, particularly among students and working people under stress. Ayurveda usually reads them as pitta rising, often with acidity, late nights, spicy food and irregular meals. For such ulcers the texts prefer cooling, soothing gandusha rather than sesame oil: liquids prepared with milk, ghee, honey or astringent, healing herbs such as yashtimadhu (liquorice) or triphala, held gently in the mouth.

Gandusha is only one part of the plan. Regular meal times, less chilli and fermented food, adequate sleep and treating constipation and acidity often matter more. It is also important to check for deficiencies such as iron, vitamin B12 and folate, which can cause recurrent ulcers. An ulcer that does not heal in two weeks, or that is painless and hard, needs urgent medical evaluation.

Why it costs what it does

Gandusha is inexpensive. It is usually taught once in clinic and then practised at home with a prescribed oil or liquid. The main costs are the consultation and the medicine. You will know them before you start.

A simple morning routine, the classical way

Ashtanga Hridaya places mouth care within a larger daily routine: waking early, cleaning the teeth, scraping the tongue, gandusha or kavala, a few drops of oil in the nose (pratimarsha nasya), oil massage before bathing and exercise to the point of light sweating. Most people cannot do all of this every day, and they do not need to. Choosing two or three of these habits and keeping them steady is more useful than attempting all of them for a week and stopping. The doctor can help you choose what suits your health and schedule.

FAQ

Questions about Gandusha

What is Gandusha?

Gandusha is a classical Ayurvedic therapy in which the mouth is filled completely with a medicated liquid such as sesame oil and held still until the eyes and nose water, then spat out.

How is Gandusha different from oil pulling?

Oil pulling involves swishing, which is closer to kavala. In gandusha the mouth is filled fully and the liquid is held without moving.

How long should Gandusha be held?

Classically, until the mouth fills with secretion and the eyes and nose start to water, usually a few minutes. The doctor will advise a time.

Can Gandusha strengthen teeth?

Classical texts describe this, and small studies suggest oil-based mouth therapies may help plaque and gum health. It does not replace dental treatment.

Who should not do Gandusha?

Children, people with swallowing problems or severe cough, and anyone with nausea, toothache, dental infection or suspicious mouth lesions should avoid it until checked.
