Yoni Dhavan and Yoni Pichu in Patna

Yoni dhavan (also called yoni prakshalana) is a gentle wash of the vagina with a lukewarm medicated decoction, and yoni pichu is the placement of a sterile tampon soaked in medicated oil or ghee in the vagina for a set time. Both are classical local procedures in Ayurvedic gynaecology (Prasuti Tantra and Stri Roga), used for certain complaints such as discharge, itching and dryness, after examination and only on medical advice.

Many women in Bihar live for years with white discharge, itching, burning or vaginal dryness without telling anyone, not even their doctor. Embarrassment, the fear of an internal examination and the belief that "this is normal for women" keep them silent. Ayurveda has a full branch of women's health, Prasuti Tantra and Stri Roga, and it includes local procedures that have been used for these complaints for centuries. This page describes two of them, Yoni dhavan and Yoni pichu, clinically and plainly, so that you can understand them before deciding whether to seek care.

What these procedures are

Yoni dhavan (yoni means the vagina and female reproductive passage; dhavan or prakshalana means washing) is a gentle cleansing of the vagina with a lukewarm, freshly prepared and filtered herbal decoction. Decoctions of astringent barks such as triphala, panchavalkala (the barks of five trees of the fig family) or similar formulations are commonly used.

Yoni pichu is the placement of a sterile cotton tampon, wrapped in gauze with a thread for removal, soaked in a medicated oil or ghee, inside the vagina. It is kept for a prescribed time and then removed. Oils and ghees chosen depend on the complaint: nourishing ones for dryness, astringent and cleansing ones for discharge.

Classical background

The classical texts describe twenty yonivyapad, disorders of the female reproductive tract, in Charaka Samhita's Chikitsasthana, with parallel discussions in Sushruta Samhita and Ashtanga Hridaya (around 7th century CE). Alongside internal medicines, they prescribe local measures called sthanika chikitsa: prakshalana (washing), pichu (medicated tampon), varti (pessaries), lepa (pastes), dhupana (fumigation) and uttara basti. Yoni dhavan and yoni pichu are therefore classical procedures, and today they are taught and studied in Prasuti Tantra and Stri Roga departments in Ayurvedic colleges across India.

How they are understood to work, and what research shows

The Ayurvedic reasoning

Ayurveda holds that apana vata governs the downward functions of the pelvis, including menstruation and childbirth, and that the health of the reproductive tract depends on balanced vata. Discharge, heaviness and itching are often read as kapha disturbance; dryness, pain during intercourse and burning as vata or pitta. Washing with astringent decoctions is thought to cleanse and tone the tissues and reduce discharge; oil or ghee pichu is thought to nourish, lubricate and calm vata locally.

Modern evidence

Research is limited and mostly small. A 2025 case series in the Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine reported on Ayurvedic medications, including local applications, for symptoms of vulvovaginal atrophy in postmenopausal women. A 2024 case report described Ayurvedic management of leucorrhoea. A 2016 randomised trial in Ayu tested Dhatakyadi varti, a vaginal pessary, in vulvovaginitis during pregnancy. These studies are encouraging but small, and they do not establish that any procedure cures infections or other disease. Importantly, many causes of discharge, such as bacterial vaginosis, trichomoniasis, gonorrhoea and chlamydia, need specific medical treatment, and some need partner treatment too.

What is normal, and when to worry

Some vaginal discharge is healthy. It is the body's way of keeping the passage clean and moist, and it changes naturally through the menstrual cycle, becoming clearer and more slippery around ovulation and thicker at other times. It also increases in pregnancy and with some contraceptives. Discharge that is clear or white, without a strong smell, itching or pain, is usually normal.

It is worth seeing a doctor when discharge changes colour to yellow, green or grey, becomes frothy or curd-like, smells strongly, or comes with itching, burning, pain during urination or intercourse, lower abdominal pain or fever. Bleeding that is not your period always needs attention. These signs do not mean something terrible, but they do mean the cause should be found before any treatment, Ayurvedic or otherwise, is chosen.

Menopause brings its own change. Falling oestrogen can thin and dry the vaginal tissues, causing burning, itching and discomfort that many women quietly accept. This is common, it is treatable, and it deserves a conversation with a doctor.

Who may be considered

After history, examination and, where needed, tests:

  • White discharge (leucorrhoea) after infections needing specific treatment have been ruled out or treated
  • Vaginal dryness, including around and after menopause
  • Itching and irritation without active infection requiring other treatment
  • Some cases of painful intercourse linked with dryness
  • As part of a wider plan for certain menstrual and fertility concerns, alongside gynaecological care

Who should not have these procedures

  • Pregnancy, unless specifically prescribed by the treating obstetrician and Ayurvedic doctor together
  • During menstruation; procedures are usually planned after bleeding stops
  • Abnormal bleeding: bleeding between periods, after intercourse or after menopause must be investigated first
  • Foul-smelling, green or frothy discharge, fever, pelvic pain or sores, until examined and treated
  • Suspected or untreated sexually transmitted infection
  • Suspicious cervical changes; cervical screening (Pap smear or HPV test) should be up to date
  • Recent pelvic surgery or delivery, unless the doctor advises otherwise
  • Adolescents and women who have not been sexually active, in whom such procedures are generally avoided or adapted

Never douche or insert herbal preparations on your own. Unsupervised douching can disturb the natural vaginal flora and increase infection risk. Continue any treatment your gynaecologist has prescribed.

Every step is explained and done only with your consent, and you may bring a companion of your choice.

The procedures, described for understanding

  1. Procedures are planned for the days after menstruation ends, with the bladder emptied beforehand.
  2. You lie on a clean, private examination table, with the body draped.
  3. For yoni dhavan, the freshly prepared, filtered, lukewarm decoction is introduced gently using sterile, single-use equipment, and allowed to drain.
  4. For yoni pichu, a sterile tampon soaked in the prescribed lukewarm oil or ghee is placed gently, with its thread left outside for easy removal.
  5. You are told how long to retain the pichu, usually until the urge to pass urine or for a set number of hours, and how to remove it.

Aftercare

Wear clean cotton underwear, avoid intercourse during the course if advised, avoid scented soaps and washes, and keep the external area clean with plain water. Diet advice depends on the complaint: for discharge, reducing heavy, sweet, fried food and curd at night; for dryness, including healthy fats such as ghee and sesame in moderation. Report any pain, fever, bleeding or unusual discharge immediately.

Questions about Yoni Dhavan and Yoni Pichu

What is Yoni Pichu?

Yoni pichu is a classical Ayurvedic gynaecological procedure in which a sterile tampon soaked in medicated oil or ghee is placed in the vagina for a prescribed time, mainly for dryness or certain types of discharge.

What is Yoni Dhavan?

Yoni dhavan, or yoni prakshalana, is a gentle wash of the vagina with a lukewarm, freshly prepared herbal decoction, done with sterile equipment under supervision.

Can Yoni Pichu treat white discharge?

It may support some cases of discharge after infections needing specific medical treatment have been ruled out. It is not a cure for infections, which may need antibiotics.

Can I do these procedures at home?

No. Unsupervised douching or inserting herbal preparations can disturb the vaginal flora and increase infection risk. They should be done only after examination and on advice.

When in the cycle are they done?

Usually in the days after menstruation ends, never during bleeding, and not in pregnancy unless specifically prescribed.

Is my privacy protected?

Yes. Consultations and procedures are private, every step is explained and done only with your consent, and you may bring a companion of your choice.
