Shiro Abhyanga in Patna

Shiro abhyanga is the Ayurvedic oil massage of the head, scalp, neck and shoulders, done with a medicated oil chosen for the person. It is the mildest of the four classical head oil therapies and is used for relaxation, better sleep, scalp dryness and tension in the head and neck.

Most of us in India grew up with champi. A mother or grandmother sitting behind you on a Sunday morning, warm coconut or mustard oil in a katori, fingers working through your hair until your eyes started to close. Shiro abhyanga is the clinical, classical version of that memory. The same instinct, refined by centuries of written practice, with an oil chosen for your constitution and complaint, and a technique that covers the scalp, temples, neck and shoulders with purpose.

What Shiro Abhyanga means

Shiras means head and abhyanga means oil application or massage. Together they describe oiling and massaging the head. In classical terms it is the first and mildest of the four murdha taila (head oil) therapies described by Vagbhata, the others being shiro seka (pouring), shiro pichu (oil-soaked cloth) and shiro basti (pooling oil in a cap), in increasing order of strength.

History: one of the best attested daily practices

Head oiling is not a later wellness invention. Charaka Samhita, in its Sutrasthana chapter on daily regimen, recommends applying oil to the head every day and lists its benefits in traditional language: it is said to prevent headache, early greying and hair fall, to strengthen the head and senses, to improve the voice and complexion and to bring sound sleep. Ashtanga Hridaya (around 7th century CE) repeats this within dinacharya, the daily routine, and adds that abhyanga should be given especially to the head, ears and feet.

Shiro abhyanga is thus a classical, pan-Indian practice. Kerala added a refined body of technique and a wide range of head oils, and the Indian household tradition of champi kept the habit alive in ordinary homes. The clinical form at an Ayurvedic centre differs mainly in the choice of medicated oil and in the doctor's decision about when it is and is not appropriate.

How it is understood to work

Classical reasoning

The texts describe the head as the seat of the senses and of prana. Vata, being dry and mobile, is said to disturb sleep, cause tension headaches and dry out the scalp and hair. Warm oil, applied with steady pressure, counters dryness and lightness and is believed to calm the mind. Choosing the oil adds further direction: cooling oils for pitta-type heat and irritability, nourishing oils for dryness and weakness.

What modern research shows

Head massage is pleasant and relaxing for most people, and massage in general has been studied for stress and muscle tension, but good-quality research on Ayurvedic shiro abhyanga specifically is limited. Reviews of Ayurvedic approaches to insomnia, published in 2026, include head therapies within multi-component plans and note that the evidence is generally small-scale. A 2026 randomised trial on nitya abhyanga (daily self-massage) with stretching looked at hamstring flexibility rather than the head, which shows that research interest in abhyanga is growing but still narrow. We present shiro abhyanga honestly as a traditional, low-risk relaxation and scalp-care therapy, not a medical treatment for any disease.

Who may enjoy and benefit from it

  • People with stress, mental fatigue or difficulty switching off at night
  • Tension in the neck, shoulders and around the temples from long screen hours
  • Dry, rough scalp and brittle hair
  • As preparation before other head therapies such as shirodhara or nasya
  • Anyone wanting a short, restorative therapy as part of a wellness routine

When to avoid or postpone

  • Acute cold, blocked nose, sinusitis, fever or sore throat; the classics say oil on the head is avoided in pratishyaya (acute rhinitis) and fever
  • Indigestion, heaviness or right after a large meal
  • Immediately after vamana or virechana, until the doctor permits it
  • Scalp infection, open wounds, active dandruff with inflammation, or recent hair transplant or head surgery
  • Recent head or neck injury, or neck pain with arm numbness or weakness, until evaluated
  • Very oily scalp with heavy kapha signs, where dry therapies may suit better

Persistent or severe headaches need a medical evaluation. Please continue any medicines your doctor prescribes.

Consultation

Even for a mild therapy, Dr. Aditya Kunal checks your sleep, stress, sinus history, scalp condition and digestion before choosing an oil. Head therapies are often part of a broader plan, especially where poor sleep and digestive problems feed each other, which is common among the people we see.

Choosing the oil

The oil may be a classical preparation such as a bhringaraja-, amalaki- or brahmi-based oil, a cooling oil for heat, or a lighter oil for those with sinus tendencies. It is warmed to a comfortable temperature.

The session, for understanding

  1. You sit upright on a chair. The therapist parts the hair and applies warm oil to the crown and scalp.
  2. The scalp is massaged with fingertips using circular and stroking movements, moving from the crown outward.
  3. Attention is given to the temples, forehead, behind the ears and the base of the skull.
  4. The neck and shoulders are massaged to release tension.
  5. You rest for a few minutes before a warm wash later, as advised.

Aftercare

Wash your hair with warm, not hot, water after the time advised, using a mild cleanser or a herbal powder. Avoid cold wind and fans directly on a wet head. A light dinner and an early night help you get the most from the session.

Mustard, coconut or medicated oil?

In Bihar, sarson tel is the household favourite, especially in winter, while coconut oil is the go-to in summer. Both have a place. Mustard oil is described as hot and penetrating, which can feel good on a cold January morning but may aggravate heat, burning eyes or scalp irritation in people with a pitta tendency. Coconut oil is cooling and suits summer and heat-prone people, but may feel heavy for those who catch colds easily. Medicated oils go a step further: herbs such as bhringaraja, amalaki, brahmi or yashtimadhu are cooked into a base oil by a long classical process, so that the oil carries their qualities. That is why, at the clinic, the oil is chosen after looking at your season, scalp, sinuses and temperament, rather than simply picking whatever is in the kitchen.

Bringing the practice home

Unlike many procedures on this site, shiro abhyanga is a classical daily routine that most healthy adults can practise at home once the doctor has suggested a suitable oil. A few minutes of warm oil on the scalp and feet before bathing, two or three times a week, is a sensible habit. Avoid it during colds and fever, wash off with warm water and never use oil so hot that it stings.

FAQ

Questions about Shiro Abhyanga

What is Shiro Abhyanga?

It is the classical Ayurvedic oil massage of the head, scalp, neck and shoulders, done with a warm medicated oil chosen for the person.

Is Shiro Abhyanga different from a normal champi?

The idea is the same, but in clinical shiro abhyanga the oil is selected by a doctor for your constitution and complaint, and the timing is checked against contraindications such as colds or indigestion.

Can I have Shiro Abhyanga when I have a cold?

It is usually avoided during an acute cold, sinus congestion or fever, as classical texts advise.

Does Shiro Abhyanga stop hair fall?

It may support scalp health, but hair fall has many causes, including thyroid problems, anaemia and stress, which should be assessed. It is not a guaranteed treatment.

How often can I do head oiling at home?

Many healthy adults can oil the head two or three times a week, using an oil the doctor suggests. Avoid it during colds and fever.
