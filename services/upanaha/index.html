उपनाह warm poultice bandage

Upanaha in Patna

Upanaha is a classical form of Ayurvedic sudation in which a warm, thick herbal paste is spread over a painful or stiff area, covered with leaves and bandaged in place for several hours. It is used mainly for vata-type pain and stiffness of the back, neck and joints, under a doctor's guidance.

Most heat therapies in Ayurveda last as long as you lie on the table. Upanaha is different. The warmth is wrapped onto you and travels home with you. A thick, warm herbal paste is spread over the painful area, covered with a leaf or cloth, and bandaged firmly so that gentle heat and medicine keep working for hours. It is one of the quieter, more patient forms of sweating therapy, and for stiff backs and cold, aching knees it is one of the most traditional.

What Upanaha is

Upanaha means "tying" or "binding on". In the context of treatment it refers to a poultice: herbs ground into a paste, usually cooked with oil or ghee, salt, fermented liquid or sour buttermilk so that it holds heat, then applied warm and bandaged. Because it causes sweating in the area without a steam chamber or direct fire, the texts classify it as a type of swedana (sudation).

Leaves of castor (eranda), arka (Calotropis) or banana were traditionally used to cover the paste, and the bandage was made of soft cloth, wool or leather depending on how much warmth was needed.

Classical roots

Upanaha is described in the earliest texts. Charaka Samhita, in its chapter on swedana, describes upanaha and advises that the poultice be applied in the evening and removed in the morning, or, in hot weather, bound for a shorter time. Sushruta Samhita and Vagbhata's Ashtanga Hridaya (around 7th century CE) group swedana into four kinds: tapa (dry heat), ushma (steam), upanaha (poultice) and drava (warm liquids), with upanaha especially recommended for vata disorders.

So unlike several Kerala-origin therapies, Upanaha is a pan-Indian classical procedure with a clearly documented textual base. Regional traditions have their own favoured pastes, such as kolakulathadi (horse gram based) for stiffness with kapha, or godhumadi (wheat based) preparations, but the method is classical.

How it is understood to work, and what research shows

Classical understanding

Vata is cold, dry, light and mobile. When it settles in joints and the lower back, the texts describe pain, stiffness and cracking that worsen with cold and improve with warmth and oil. Upanaha counters every one of those qualities at once: warmth, unctuousness from oil or ghee, weight from the thick paste and stability from the bandage. When ama (undigested residue) is also present, with heaviness and swelling, drier and more penetrating pastes are chosen instead.

Modern research

There is some published clinical work. A randomised comparative trial published in Ayu (2019) from KLE's Shri BMK Ayurveda Mahavidyalaya in Belagavi studied upanaha sweda in low back pain (katigraha). It selected the type of poultice according to whether ama was present, using panchakoladi upanaha first in those with ama and godhumadi upanaha afterwards. A 2013 report in the same journal described multimodal Ayurvedic management of knee osteoarthritis in which local therapies were part of the plan. These studies are small, and combined treatments make it hard to isolate the effect of Upanaha alone. They support its use as one component of care, not as a cure for any spinal or joint disease.

Who may benefit

  • Chronic low back pain and stiffness, after serious causes have been ruled out
  • Neck stiffness from posture and long desk hours
  • Knee, ankle and shoulder stiffness that feels worse in cold weather
  • Old injuries with residual stiffness
  • Muscle spasm and cramp-like pain in a specific area

Who should avoid Upanaha

  • Joints that are hot, red and very swollen, or pain with fever, which need medical evaluation
  • Recent injury in the first days, when swelling is fresh
  • Open wounds, skin infection, eczema or fragile skin in the area
  • Areas with reduced sensation, such as diabetic neuropathy, where heat may not be felt
  • Varicose veins or suspected deep vein thrombosis in the limb
  • Pregnancy, unless the doctor specifically approves the area and medicines
  • Pain with loss of bladder or bowel control, leg weakness or numbness, which is a medical emergency

Upanaha does not replace evaluation by a spine or orthopaedic specialist when one is needed. Continue prescribed medicines.

Upanaha at Sabka Ved Wellness

1. Assessment

Dr. Aditya Kunal asks where and when the pain started, whether it radiates, what worsens and eases it, and reviews any X-ray or MRI reports. He also checks digestion, because in Ayurveda pain with heaviness and poor appetite suggests ama, and the choice of paste changes accordingly.

2. Preparing the poultice

The herbal powders are mixed with oil, salt and a sour or fermented liquid and gently heated into a thick, warm paste. Fresh leaves are cleaned and warmed. Preparation takes time and is done for each patient individually.

3. Application, for understanding

  1. The area may first be oiled and warmed lightly.
  2. The warm paste is spread in a thick layer, with the therapist checking the temperature against the skin.
  3. Leaves are placed over the paste, followed by a soft cloth bandage tied firmly but not tightly.
  4. You are told how long to keep it on: sometimes a few hours, sometimes overnight, depending on the season and your skin.
  5. On removal, the area is wiped with warm water and kept covered.

If you feel burning, itching, tingling or tightness, remove the bandage and contact us. This is not a do-it-yourself method.

4. Aftercare and diet

Avoid cold water on the area, cold drinks and exposure to cold wind while on treatment. Favour warm, freshly cooked food; reduce curd, cold leftovers and heavy fried snacks, which the texts say aggravate vata and kapha.

Why Upanaha suits life in Patna

Back and knee stiffness have a seasonal rhythm in Bihar. December and January bring damp cold and fog, and many people notice their joints are worst in the early morning, loosening slowly as the day warms up. The monsoon months bring humidity and heaviness, which the texts link with kapha joining vata in the joints. Upanaha can be adapted to both: oily, nourishing pastes for dry, cold, cracking joints; drier, more penetrating pastes with horse gram or similar ingredients for heavy, swollen ones.

It also suits people who cannot spend long hours at a centre. Because the bandage continues to work at home, a single visit delivers several hours of local warmth. That makes it practical for working people and for elderly patients who find lying on a treatment table for long periods uncomfortable. The trade-off is responsibility: you have to look after the bandage, keep it dry and remove it on time, and the therapist will explain exactly how before you leave.

FAQ

Questions about Upanaha

What is Upanaha sweda?

Upanaha is a classical Ayurvedic poultice in which a warm herbal paste is spread over a painful area, covered with leaves and bandaged so that heat and medicine continue to act for hours.

Is Upanaha mentioned in classical texts?

Yes. Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita and Ashtanga Hridaya all describe it as a type of swedana, especially for vata disorders.

How long should the Upanaha bandage stay on?

It depends on season, skin and the paste used. Classical texts describe overnight application, but the doctor may advise a few hours. Remove it if you feel burning or itching.

Does Upanaha help back pain?

A small randomised trial in Ayu (2019) studied upanaha in low back pain. It is used as part of a plan for pain and stiffness, but it is not a cure for spinal disease.

Can diabetics have Upanaha?

With caution. Areas with reduced sensation need careful temperature checks or a different therapy. The doctor will decide.
